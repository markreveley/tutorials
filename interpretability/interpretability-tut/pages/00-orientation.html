Module 0 · Claims and the protocol

A claim about a model’s internals is a hypothesis

What it means to say a neuron, a direction, or a head “does” something, and what it would take to have shown it.

Every explanation is a claim about the future

“Neuron 2928 in layer 2 fires at the start of a sentence.” That sentence looks like a description. It is a prediction. It says that on text the neuron has never seen, its activation will be high on sentence-initial tokens and low elsewhere, and if that is not what happens, the sentence is false. The whole discipline of this course is in treating interpretability claims that way: as hypotheses that predict something, that could have come out otherwise, and that have not been demonstrated until they did come out that way on data chosen before anyone looked.

This is the same move you made in the statistics course when you named the estimand before computing anything, and in the evals course when you validated a grader on a held-out set. The objects are different. The habit is identical.

Why this is the hard barrier

The toolchain, the linear algebra, and the transformer anatomy are all learnable from documentation. Rigor is not, because the failure is invisible from inside. When you look at the ten inputs that most excite a unit, your eye finds the pattern, and the pattern feels like understanding. The feeling is real. The understanding may not be. In 2026 two researchers who worked on the same OpenAI neuron-explanation project disagreed in public about exactly this. Nick Cammarata’s essay opens: “I think I have good news about the nature of neural networks,” and argues that “a network’s own mechanisms are its legible building blocks” [Cammarata 2026]. Leo Gao’s reply two days later grants the observation and refuses the conclusion: “To be able to claim that we understand a neuron, it’s insufficient for things to look kinda nice. If you can’t simulate the neuron in a way that doesn’t degrade the model, you don’t really understand the neuron” [Gao 2026].

Both are careful people. Both looked at the same kind of evidence. The disagreement is about what counts as demonstrated, and that is a question you can settle for yourself, on a laptop, with the methods in this course. The course does not take a side on how clean networks are. It takes a side on method: a test written before the look is worth more than any number of looks.

What “demonstrated” means here

A demonstrated explanation of a unit has four properties, and the rest of the course is a tour of how to get each one.

PropertyWhat it rules outLessons
It predicts behaviour on held-out dataFitting a story to the examples that suggested it01, 04
Its score was defined before the runChoosing the metric that flatters the result01, 05
It beats controls that an empty claim would passScores that any unit or any rule would get05, 07
Its failure would have been reportedThe file drawer13

Notice what is not on the list. It does not say the explanation must be causal, or complete, or stated in natural language, or that the unit must be monosemantic. Those are stronger claims with their own tests (lessons 10 and 12). A claim can be modest and demonstrated, or ambitious and not. Modest and demonstrated is a contribution.

Two models you can run

Everything in the labs runs on a CPU in under two minutes per command. For vision, the model is torchvision’s GoogLeNet, the InceptionV1 architecture, loaded with its ImageNet weights [torchvision]. For language, it is GPT-2 small, 124 million parameters, loaded through TransformerLens so that every activation has a name. Lesson 06 will make an important point about the vision model: its weights are not the weights the Distill papers studied, so a unit number from a paper does not name the same unit here. That turned a planned replication into a different and better exercise.

claim → prediction on unseen data → scoring function → controls → threshold → run → report (pass or fail) → what it does and does not establish
In interpretability work

When a colleague shows you a neuron and says what it does, your first question is not “is that right?” It is “what did you predict, on what data, and what would a random neuron have scored?” If there is no answer, there is no result yet; there is a lead.

Pitfall

Treating the strength of a pattern in top examples as evidence of its generality. The top examples were selected for being extreme; they are the one place a unit is guaranteed to look clean. Gao’s post describes a neuron whose strongest examples are dogs on bicycles, whose weaker ones are just dogs, and asks whether you can therefore conclude it is a dogs-on-bicycles neuron: “I argue we cannot” [Gao 2026].

Check your understanding

A write-up says: “We found a neuron that detects dates. Its top 20 activations are all on year tokens like 1911 and 2007.” What has been shown, and what has not?

Reveal the reasoning

Shown: on the inputs that most excite the neuron, year tokens dominate. Not shown: that year tokens generally excite it (recall), that non-year tokens do not (precision), that the neuron’s weaker activations follow the same rule, or that a random neuron scored against the same rule would not look similar. The claim is a hypothesis with a promising lead; lesson 04 turns it into a test.

Practice

Write down one claim you have read or heard about what some unit in some model does. Rewrite it as a prediction: on what data, what number, how large. Then write the control: what would also produce that number if the claim were empty?

What to look for

Most claims become much narrower when written as predictions, and that is the point. “Detects dates” might become “activation above 1.0 on at least 80% of four-digit year tokens in held-out text, and on fewer than 5% of other tokens.” The control is a random neuron of the same layer scored the same way.
