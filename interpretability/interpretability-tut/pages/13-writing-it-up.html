Module 4 · Features and the write-up

Writing it up: the demonstrated-explanation checklist and the Gao critique

How to report a result so that it is a contribution, how to report a failure so that it is one too, and the exact shape of the 2026 disagreement about simulation scores.

Reporting a negative result

This course contains two. The named-unit vision test (lesson 06) failed because the units named in the paper do not exist in these weights; the probe test (lesson 08) failed because the threshold ignored the base rate. Each is reported with its full table, the pre-registered verdict, and the diagnosis. Neither is softened into “partial success.” The template for a negative result is the same as for a positive one with two additions: the diagnosis (what, specifically, the failure is evidence of: a wrong model, a wrong threshold, a wrong claim) and the revised protocol you would run next, labelled as not yet run.

The reason to be strict about this is in Nanda’s guide: “The default state of the world is that your research is false, because doing research is hard,” and “Excitement is evidence of bullshit: Generally, most true results are not exciting, but a fair amount of false results are” [Nanda 2025]. A field in which negative results are reported is one where positive results can be believed. A blog post reports what worked; a contribution reports what was tested.

The Gao critique, exactly

Gao’s post makes three moves, and a write-up should be able to say which of them it answers.

The pattern in the examples is not the explanation. “Even if there is a simple pattern that matches the activating examples of a neuron, that still doesn’t mean that the explanation is correct,” for two reasons: the pattern may not be sufficient (“perhaps not all examples that fit the pattern also activate the neuron”), and “there might actually be really important information in the activation magnitudes” [Gao 2026]. Lessons 03 and 04 are the response: score on all held-out tokens, report recall as well as precision, and say which regime the score covers.

Simulation, with the right score. “If you can’t simulate the neuron in a way that doesn’t degrade the model, you don’t really understand the neuron.” The procedure is a rubric that assigns a simulated value to each input, “substituting neuron values with the simulated values,” and the test is downstream: “If this process heavily degrades the model, then our explanation was wrong.” Gao argues that the objections to simulation scoring are objections to the original correlation version, “fixed basically entirely by (a) measuring downstream KL instead and (b) scoring on a large dataset.” The course’s correlation score is the original version; the KL variant is the natural next step and the capstone asks you to describe how you would run it.

The field’s failure mode. “Historically, the field of interpretability has often gotten stuck because of a lack of rigor, focusing on things that look cool even when those things oversell our actual level of understanding.”

Cammarata’s reply in advance

The essay has a section titled “On Simulation Scores” that anticipates the second move. Its argument is that “Simulation scores often end up measuring where attention puts information, which is a question about attention motifs rather than about the neuron,” that “Simulation also rewards fine details of weak activations that matter little for understanding what a neuron does,” and that “language models are poor at outputting precise activation values even when they understand what’s going on.” Its conclusion: “So I’d rather rely on normal science than on any one metric. Simulation might eventually become one useful score among several, but later, once we understand networks well, and probably with simulators trained for the job. But I don’t think they’re a good place to start” [Cammarata 2026]. Elsewhere it says the 2023 metrics were “rewarding explanations that could simulate activations rather than ones that were right, which often come apart,” and that it no longer thinks the activation-reconstruction task “is a great metric for explanations.”

Read the two side by side and the disagreement is narrow and empirical. Both agree that top examples are insufficient. Both agree that predictions from the explanation alone, on new inputs, are the test (the essay’s agent-written positive controls and “distant concept” texts are such predictions, with their own numbers: the distant texts “fired their neurons strongly about 1% of the time, and 179 of the 183 neurons never fired on them at all”). They disagree about whether a numeric simulation score, and specifically one that penalises errors in weak activations, is the right arbiter now. Gao’s KL proposal is designed to answer the essay’s objection directly: if weak activations and attention-placed information do not matter to the output, KL will not punish getting them wrong. Whether that is true is a measurement nobody in this course has run; the capstone’s last section asks you to design it.

What separates a blog post from a contribution

Not the length, the venue, or the model size. A blog post can be a contribution and a paper can be a blog post. The difference is whether the reader can tell what would have happened if the claim were false. A contribution states the test before the result, scores on data the claim did not touch, shows the controls, fixes the threshold first, and reports failures at the same resolution as successes. The 2026 essay calls itself “an informal research essay and working hypothesis” and says “A technical companion paper will offer a more detailed account”; the critique says “I have a post on this in the works” and “Hopefully I'll be able to share something more concrete soon.” Both are telling you where they are on this scale. Do the same.

In interpretability work

Put the protocol, verbatim and dated, at the top of the write-up, followed by the results table, followed by what was not tested. Anything you learned after the run goes in a section labelled exploratory. This order is unnatural to write and natural to read.

Pitfall

Reporting the control as a footnote. The control rate belongs in the same sentence as the pass rate; lesson 07’s “12/12 against 22/100” is one number, not two.

Check your understanding

A draft says: “Neuron N is a date neuron (explanation score 0.81). Ablating it changed the loss by 0.002 nats, so it is not important.” Name two problems.

Reveal the reasoning

First, the score’s data is unstated: 0.81 on which excerpts, against what control, with what base rate. Second, the ablation result is read backwards: 0.002 nats for a single neuron among 36,864 may be large or small, and Gao’s footnote notes that “Each individual neuron has a very small effect on the entire network, so we’d expect the number to be very small in some absolute sense”; compare against random neurons’ ablation effects before calling it unimportant. A third, if you want it: “date neuron” has become a noun phrase, which is how a hypothesis turns into a fact in the retelling.

Practice

Take any one result from this course’s transcript and write it up in the order the lesson gives: protocol, results table with controls, not-tested table, exploratory section. Then write the same result as a two-sentence blog-post claim, and list what the second version lets a reader get wrong.

What to look for

The two-sentence version almost always drops the control and the held-out qualifier, and often promotes the unit to a noun (“the curve detector,” “the induction head”). The full version is longer and less quotable; it is the one a replicator can use.
