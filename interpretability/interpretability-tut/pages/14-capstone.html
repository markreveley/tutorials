Module 5 · Capstone

Capstone: pick a neuron by a documented procedure, pre-register, run, report

The whole protocol on one GPT-2 small MLP neuron, end to end, with the reference run as a worked example and your own run as the deliverable.

The task

Choose one MLP neuron of GPT-2 small by a procedure you write down before you run it. Write the pre-registration (Appendix A) before you look at the neuron on held-out text. Score the explanation on held-out tokens with at least two controls. Report the result in the order of lesson 13, whichever way it came out. The deliverable is a short document; a template is in labs/report-template.md.

The reference run that the course used for lessons 04 and 05 is one instance of this task with eight rules at once. Its documented procedure, from the script’s docstring:

1. Tokenise labs/data/corpus.txt; even 128-token chunks are the screening split,
   odd chunks are held out and are not touched until step 4.
2. For each rule (a deterministic token predicate), find the neuron in every
   layer whose activation correlates best with the rule on the screening split.
   This is where selection bias enters: 12 x 3072 neurons were searched.
3. Pre-register, per rule: held-out Pearson r >= 0.5, AND r above the 95th
   percentile of (a) 200 random neurons from the same layer and (b) 500 shuffled
   copies of the rule's labels.
4. Score on the held-out split and print everything, including failures.

Your procedure must differ in at least one of these

The point of the capstone is that you make the choices and own them. Pick at least one:

  • A new rule. Something contextual rather than token-local: “token inside a quotation,” “second token of a sentence,” “a number that is a quantity rather than a date.” Layer-0 neurons will not carry these; your screening will have to find deeper ones, and your held-out scores will be lower. That is the interesting regime.
  • A new corpus. Write 2,000 tokens of your own prose of a different kind (code comments, dialogue, lists) and use the course corpus as the held-out set, or the reverse. This tests distribution shift, which the reference run does not.
  • A different selection. Instead of the best-correlated neuron, take the neuron with the highest activation variance in layer 6 and try to explain it from its quantile bands (lesson 03), then pre-register before scoring. This is the harder, more realistic direction: neuron first, explanation second.
  • A baseline control. Add a competing explanation to the controls (lesson 05) and require the claim to beat it by a pre-registered margin.

The worked example, read as a capstone report

Protocol: the four steps above, with the threshold and controls fixed in the script before it was run. Unit: chosen by step 2. Held-out: 12 chunks, 1,536 tokens, never decoded before scoring. Scoring: Pearson r on all held-out tokens. Result:

         rule layer neuron  train r held-out r  rand95  shuf95 |rand|max  base rate  verdict
 after_period     2   2928    0.860      0.845   0.125   0.042     0.560      0.103  demonstrated

Not tested: whether the neuron does anything else (r² = 0.71, so 29% of its variance is unexplained by the rule); whether the model uses it (no intervention; lesson 10 would patch it); whether it holds on text unlike the corpus; whether its weak activations follow the rule (the score is dominated by the strong tail). Exploratory: lesson 03’s quantile bands suggest the weak region is also sentence starts, but that was a look, not a test. Revised protocol, not yet run: add a baseline control (“token is capitalised”), add a text of a different genre as a second held-out set, and pre-register a precision/recall operating point at activation > 0.5.

The extension the 2026 debate asks for

Lesson 13 left one test undesigned: Gao’s downstream-KL version. Here is its shape, for you to fill in and, if you have the time, run.

  1. Take the held-out chunks. Run the model normally and record the output distribution at every position.
  2. Run again with a hook on blocks.2.mlp.hook_post that replaces neuron 2928’s activation at every position with the value your explanation predicts: for a rule-form explanation, the neuron’s mean activation on rule-positive screening tokens where the rule fires, and its mean on rule-negative tokens elsewhere.
  3. Measure the mean KL divergence from the normal output distribution to the substituted one, over held-out positions.
  4. Controls: the same substitution for the random neurons of the random-unit control; and substituting the neuron’s overall mean everywhere (mean ablation), which is the KL an explanation with no content would produce.
  5. Pre-register: the explanation’s KL is below the mean-ablation KL by a stated fraction, and below the median of the random-neuron substitutions.

This is the test Gao says would have flagged the cat-and-car explanation of 4e:55, and the one Cammarata says rewards details that do not matter. Both predictions are checkable on this neuron in a few minutes of CPU. If you run it, report it the same way.

How the capstone is read

The readiness review on the next page gives the rubric. The short version: a reader should be able to re-run your test from the protocol alone, should see every control beside the score, and should know from your not-tested table exactly what your claim does not cover. A negative result that meets those three is a pass. A positive result that does not is not.

In interpretability work

The habit this capstone trains is the one the advice behind this course named as the barrier: the explanation that “cannot predict a neuron’s activations on held-out data has not been demonstrated.” You now have the procedure, the controls, the code, and two worked examples, one of which failed. Write the test before you look.

Pitfall

Iterating on the rule against the held-out split. The first time you score a revised rule on the same held-out chunks, they stop being held out. Keep a third split for the final score, or write the revised rule as a new pre-registration and score it on new text.

Check your understanding

You chose the neuron-first direction, wrote an explanation from the quantile bands, and your held-out r is 0.31 with rand95 = 0.14 and shuf95 = 0.05. Pass or fail, and what is the report?

Reveal the reasoning

Fail against a 0.5 threshold, and the report says so, with the three numbers. The diagnosis: the explanation is above both nulls, so it captures something specific to this neuron, but explains about 10% of its variance; the neuron is either broader than the rule or the rule is the wrong shape (precision/recall would tell which). The revised protocol proposes a second rule and a new held-out split, labelled not yet run. That document is a contribution: it says what was tested and what a reader should not believe.

Practice

Run the reference table once more as a baseline, then do the capstone with your own procedure. Use labs/protocol.py for the pre-registration and labs/explanation_score.py for the scoring, and write the report with labs/report-template.md.

python labs/reference/neuron_score.py --all
What to look for

Your held-out scores for contextual rules will be well below the token-local ones in the table; 0.3 to 0.6 is the range to expect for a rule that is roughly right about a deeper neuron. The report is judged on the protocol and the controls, not on the size of r.
