Module 4 · Features and the write-up

Features from sparse autoencoders, and how their explanations are scored

What an SAE feature is, how Neuronpedia presents one, what its explanation score means, and why the 2026 debate is partly about whether to train the second model at all.

The second model

A sparse autoencoder is trained to reconstruct a layer’s activations through a bottleneck that is wider than the layer but sparse: an encoder maps the activation vector to many latents of which only a few are active, and a decoder maps those back. Each latent is a direction in activation space plus a sparse activation, and the claim is that these directions are a better unit of analysis than neurons. Anthropic’s 2023 paper did this on “a one-layer transformer with a 512-neuron MLP layer” and reported that “Sparse autoencoders produce interpretable features that are effectively invisible in the neuron basis. We find features (e.g., one firing on Hebrew script) which are not active in any of the top dataset examples for any of the neurons” [Towards Monosemanticity]. Their blinded human rubric gave the median neuron a 0 (“our annotator could not even form a hypothesis of what the neuron could represent!”) and the median feature a 12.

OpenAI’s 2024 paper scaled the method with a k-sparse (TopK) activation that fixes the number of active latents directly, trained “a 16 million latent autoencoder on GPT-4 activations for 40 billion tokens,” and proposed quality metrics beyond reconstruction: “whether certain hypothesized features were recovered, whether downstream effects are sparse, and whether features can be explained with both high precision and recall” [Gao et al. 2024]. One of those is downstream loss: “How good is the language model loss if the residual stream latent is replaced with the autoencoder reconstruction of that latent?” For their largest autoencoder, substituting it into GPT-4 gave a loss “corresponding to 10% of the pretraining compute of GPT-4,” which is a candid way to report how much the reconstruction loses.

A feature is a claim like any other

For the purposes of this course, an SAE feature is a direction chosen by an unsupervised search, and its explanation is scored exactly the way a neuron’s is: a simulator (model or rule) predicts the feature’s activation from the explanation alone on held-out tokens, and the prediction is scored against the truth. The 2023 paper used a computational proxy for one of its features, Arabic script, and reports “a Pearson correlation of 0.74 between the activity of our feature and the activity of the Arabic script proxy (thresholded at 0), over a dataset of 40 million tokens” [Towards Monosemanticity]. That is lesson 04’s score with a rule as the simulator, at scale. Everything lesson 05 said about controls applies: a random feature scored against the same proxy, the proxy shuffled, and a baseline explanation.

Two further tests exist for features that do not exist for neurons, because a feature comes with a reconstruction. The reconstruction error says how much of the layer the whole dictionary explains; the downstream loss says how much the model cares. A feature can have a clean explanation and belong to a dictionary that wrecks the model when spliced in, and then the explanation is of something the model does not quite compute.

Looking one up

Neuronpedia describes itself as “an open source interpretability platform” and hosts, among other releases, residual-stream sparse autoencoders for GPT-2 small, with a search over latents by explanation text or by running custom text through a model [Neuronpedia]. A feature page typically shows its top-activating examples, an auto-generated explanation, that explanation’s score, activation histograms, and the tokens it promotes and suppresses. Reading such a page with this course’s habits:

A network lookup is optional for this course and nothing in the labs depends on it. The exercise is to read the page as a protocol with fields filled in by someone else, and to notice which fields are empty.

Second model or native neurons

The 2026 essay’s central methodological claim is that the second model may be unnecessary and sometimes harmful. It describes two approaches, “rapid normal science on the network itself” versus starting “from a list of hypothesized features” and training a decomposition, and argues the field “fell back to the second because neural networks seemed to be tangled knots” [Cammarata 2026]. Its vision experiment retrains InceptionV1 with mixed3b squeezed to 10 channels and trains an SAE on the squeezed layer: “We don’t see anything like clean curve neurons!” It cites Gorton’s SAE study of InceptionV1 early vision [Gorton 2024] and argues the SAE curves there “decompose into existing curve-responsive neurons.” And it reports, for Qwen3-32B, “roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with small related offshoots; another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood,” with the caveat that the number depends on thresholds for weak activations and on “what you view as ‘one thing’.”

Gao’s reply accepts the observation and keeps superposition as the explanation for what circuits look like: “My current best explanation for this is that concepts are stored in superposition, and MLPs do computation on those features in superposition, by doing more than one computation per neuron” [Gao 2026]. The two positions make different predictions that the methods in this course can test on a small model: if native neurons are clean, a rule-form explanation of a neuron should reach a high held-out score and substituting the simulated activations should barely change the model; if features are in superposition, SAE latents should score higher than the best neuron on the same proxy, and splicing the dictionary in should hurt less than ablating the neuron. Neither side disputes that the test is the right arbiter. That is the course’s position too, and it is why the capstone scores a native neuron rather than a feature: it is the cheaper experiment and the one the essay invites.

In interpretability work

Treat an SAE feature’s explanation score as you would a neuron’s from Bills et al.: read which excerpts it was computed on, and remember Huang et al.’s result that a high top-and-random score is compatible with an F1 of 0.56 in a full observational audit. The platform tells you the score; the protocol tells you what it is a score of.

Pitfall

“Feature splitting” read as discovery. The 2023 paper reports that “Features appear to ‘split’ as we increase autoencoder size,” one base64 feature becoming three. Whether three features are more true than one is a question about the model, not about the autoencoder, and the essay’s complaint about SAEs that “fracture” neurons is the same question from the other side. A split that improves held-out prediction of the model’s behaviour is evidence; one that only improves the dictionary’s reconstruction is not.

Check your understanding

A feature page shows an explanation “mentions of rivers” with score 0.83 and a histogram where 95% of nonzero activations are below a tenth of the maximum. What does the score probably reflect, and what would you run?

Reveal the reasoning

With that histogram, a top-and-random score of 0.83 almost certainly reflects the top excerpts, where river mentions dominate, and says little about the weak 95%. Run lesson 04’s procedure: a rule for “river mention” scored on all tokens of a held-out text, with a random-feature control and the shuffle. If the all-token score is far below 0.83, the explanation covers the feature’s strong tail only, and the write-up should say which regime it explains.

Practice

Without a network, write the protocol (Appendix A) for testing one claimed SAE feature of your choice from a paper, as if you had the dictionary weights. Name the proxy rule, the held-out data, the two controls, the reconstruction check, and the downstream-loss check. If you have a network, open one GPT-2 small feature on Neuronpedia and fill the protocol from its page, marking every field the page leaves empty.

What to look for

The field most often empty is the control: feature pages report a score but rarely what a random feature scores on the same explanation. The second is the excerpt mix the score used. A protocol with those two fields filled would let you compare the feature against a native neuron found by lesson 05’s screening on the same rule, which is the experiment the 2026 debate is asking for.
