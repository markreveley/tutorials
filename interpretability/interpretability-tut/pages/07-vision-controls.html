Module 2 · Vision replication on a CPU

Vision II: controls for the vision claim, and what replication established

Random channels, rotated stimuli, and the line comparison, read as a sceptic would read them. Then the honest summary.

What 72% means

H1 (preferred orientation stable across radius and thickness) passed for 100% of candidates and 72% of random channels. A control pass rate that high says the hypothesis is weak on its own: most channels in an early vision layer are orientation-tuned to something, and a thin dark arc has an orientation, so most channels’ preferred angle survives a change of radius. H1 is a necessary condition, not a discriminating one. It was pre-registered anyway because its failure would have been decisive.

H2 (silent at the opposite orientation) is where curve detectors separate from edge detectors: 47% of random channels passed. H4 (arc beats line by 1.5×) is the other half of the same distinction: 46%. H3 (contrast inversion) at 42% tests the paper’s cosmetic-invariance claim. The joint rate is the number to quote: 22% of random channels pass all four, against 100% of candidates. If the candidates had been random channels dressed up by screening, you would expect their joint rate to be near 22%, not 100%. That gap is the result.

But notice what 22% also says: roughly a fifth of mixed3b channels behave like curve detectors on these tests without having been selected for it. Either the family is much larger than twelve, or the four tests are not strict enough to exclude units that are curve-like without being curve detectors (the paper’s own candidates for that role are “double curve detectors” and units that respond to angles). A sceptic would say: your tests admit a fifth of the layer; your candidates are the fifth that also respond strongly. The reply is that the screening score was peak × selectivity, so “respond strongly and selectively” is exactly what was selected, and the held-out tests asked four independent questions the screening did not. Both are right. The honest statement is that the tests discriminate curve-like from non-curve-like channels, and the screening picks the strongest curve-like ones.

The rotated-stimulus control is built in

A classic control for a claimed detector is to show the same stimulus transformed in a way the claim says should matter. For orientation the transform is rotation, and the tuning curve already contains it: every orientation from 0° to 350° is the same arc rotated. H2 reads one point of that curve, the 180° rotation, where the arc’s tangent is unchanged and its curvature sign is flipped. The paper describes the analogous phenomenon in its lower layers: conv2d2 curve units “also weakly activate to a range on the opposite side, 180 degrees away. We call this secondary range echoes” [Curve Detectors]. In the authoring run, all twelve mixed3b candidates had an opposite-orientation response of 0.00 of peak (one was −0.05, below baseline). No echoes at this layer, in these weights, at these stimulus sizes.

What was not established

Be exact about scope, because the paper is. Its claims are causality, generality, purity, and family. Of the seven lines of evidence, this replication ran two (synthetic examples and feature visualisation) and a fragment of a third (the tuning curve, but on synthetic rather than rotated dataset examples). It did not run dataset examples at all, because the course ships no natural images; so the claim that these channels fire on curves in photographs, which is what makes them detectors rather than responders, is untested here. The paper’s “Purity” claim, that curve detectors “are not polysemantic and they have no meaningful secondary function,” needs the dataset analysis and attribution it was built on; the synthetic tests cannot address it. And the paper itself reports fragility: “tiny changes in orientation or curvature can cause dramatic changes in activations, which indicate that curve detectors are fragile and non-robust.” The held-out tests used a 20° tolerance and two new radii; they did not map the wisps.

Why this matters for the 2026 argument

The essay’s argument about superposition leans on this layer. It retrains InceptionV1 with mixed3b “cut from 480 to 10 channels,” reports that the channels stay interpretable but coarser, with “one ‘curves & loops’ neuron instead of ten curve detectors,” and tests whether a sparse autoencoder on the squeezed layer recovers ten curve detectors: “We don’t see anything like clean curve neurons!” [Cammarata 2026]. Its table gives the original ten detectors’ tuning widths as “24–56° wide and silent at the opposite angle,” measured as “the response at the centre of the image to a thin dark arc drawn at 180 angles and two curvatures.” That is the same measurement as the labs, on different weights. “Silent at the opposite angle” is H2, and in this run it held for every candidate. You now have the apparatus to check one piece of a 2026 argument with your own forward passes, which is the point of the vision half of the course.

In interpretability work

Write the “not established” table before the “established” one. Reviewers find the gaps anyway; a write-up that names them first is read as careful, and one that omits them is read as either naive or selective.

Pitfall

Quoting the pass rate without the control rate. “12/12 passed” is meaningless without “22/100 random channels passed the same tests.” The pair is the result.

Check your understanding

You add a fifth held-out test: response to a filled disc of the same radius. Which way should a curve detector go, and what does the paper say about fill?

Reveal the reasoning

The paper reports that curves “are invariant to fill” in its synthetic experiments, so a curve detector should respond to a filled disc’s edge about as it does to the arc at the matching orientation; a unit that drops to zero on fill would be a thin-line detector rather than a curve detector. Pre-register the direction and a tolerance before rendering the discs. Note that a disc has every orientation on its boundary, so the orientation-selective response comes from the part of the edge that falls in the receptive field.

Practice

Raise the random-channel control from 100 to 300 and record the joint pass rate. Then implement labs/curve_detector.py; its tests include a synthetic line detector that must fail H2 and H4 and a synthetic curve detector that must pass all four.

python labs/reference/curve_tuning.py --random 300
What to look for

The joint control rate should move only a few points with 300 channels; if it moves a lot, 100 was too few and the protocol should have said 300. The tests for evaluate_candidate are the specification of what “curve detector” means in this course: write them down in words before coding, and compare your words with the paper’s.
