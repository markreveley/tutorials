Module 1 · Evidence that misleads and evidence that counts

Max-activating examples and why they mislead

The top-k view is where every investigation starts and where most wrong explanations are born. Quantile sampling is the fix.

What the top-k view shows

Take neuron 2928 in layer 2 of GPT-2 small, run the course corpus through the model, and sort the 3,200 tokens by the neuron’s activation. The ten largest, from the authoring run:

The bracketed token is the one whose activation is shown. Every one of the ten follows a full stop or a paragraph break. A human looking at this writes “fires at the start of a sentence,” and that is a good hypothesis. It is also exactly the kind of evidence that Gao’s post warns about: “Top activations can often give a misleading impression of how monosemantic a neurons is” [Gao 2026]. Three distinct things can go wrong.

Three ways the top-k view misleads

Selection bias. The ten tokens were chosen because they are extreme. Any neuron, including one with no coherent function, has ten most extreme tokens, and with a vocabulary of 50,257 and a corpus of a few thousand tokens, the extremes of a noisy unit will often share a superficial feature by chance. The top-k view cannot distinguish a real rule from the tail of a distribution.

Polysemanticity hidden in the tail. Suppose the neuron fires on sentence starts and on something else at half the strength. The second thing never reaches the top ten. The Curve Detectors paper is explicit that its quilts of examples across the activation range exist for this reason: “Quilts of images reveal patterns across a wide range of activations, but they can be misleading” [Curve Detectors]. Their 3b:379 “fires in just 11% of cases across the dataset,” and most of those firings are weak.

The top-k illusion of sufficiency. Even if every top example fits the pattern, the pattern may not be sufficient: Gao’s footnote puts it as “X matches Y is not commutative!” The neuron may fire on all the top sentence starts and still be silent on most sentence starts. Only a test that scores the pattern on every token, including the ones where the neuron is quiet, can tell.

Quantile sampling

The fix is to look at random examples from fixed bands of the activation distribution instead of the extreme. The same script prints four bands; here are the first two and the last:

For this neuron the picture holds up in the bands: the 90th to 99th percentile is still sentence starts, and the bottom half is mid-sentence tokens. That is reassuring but it is still looking, not testing. Lesson 04 scores the rule on every held-out token, and the sentence-start rule gets a held-out correlation of 0.845 against a random-neuron 95th percentile of 0.125. The looking suggested the rule; the scoring is what demonstrates it.

Two things to read off the summary line above the top ten. The neuron is positive on 16.9% of tokens but above half its maximum on only 7.1%, so a rule that predicts “fires” for 10% of tokens is in the right regime. And the mean activation is 0.033 with standard deviation 0.417, which is what a sparse unit looks like: a correlation between a 0/1 rule and this distribution is dominated by whether the rule finds the 7% of strong tokens, which is why lesson 04 also reports the base rate beside every score.

What the 2026 essay does with weak activations

Cammarata’s essay reads the whole distribution rather than the top, and reports that weak activations change the picture: “How far to go into weak activations is a big factor here,” with the Qwen3-32B audit’s own caveat that the share of neurons with one job “varies depending on how you set the threshold for counting weak activations, depth, density of sub-circuits, and what you view as ‘one thing’” [Cammarata 2026]. Gao’s reply puts weight on the same region for the opposite reason: “My best guess from all the results I've seen is that ablating small neuron activations quite substantially degrades model performance, so I don’t think we get to ignore weak activations completely” [Gao 2026]. Both agree you must look below the top. They disagree about whether the weak region needs to be predicted to count as understood. The course’s position is procedural: decide before you look which region your score covers, and say so.

In interpretability work

Before you write what a unit does, print the activation summary (fraction positive, fraction above half max) and one random example from each quantile band. If the bands disagree with the top ten, you have two hypotheses, not one.

Pitfall

Mistaking a dataset artefact for a neuron property. The corpus here is 3,270 tokens of prose with many paragraph breaks. A neuron that looks like a “paragraph break” neuron on this corpus may look different on code. Held-out data from the same distribution tests generalisation within the distribution; it does not test the distribution.

Check your understanding

A unit’s top 20 examples are all chemical formulas. Random samples from its 50th–90th percentile band are mostly ordinary nouns with a few formulas. What are the two hypotheses, and what single number from lesson 04 would separate them?

Reveal the reasoning

Hypothesis A: a formula detector with a long weak tail of unrelated firing. Hypothesis B: a broader unit (say, “technical noun”) whose extreme is formulas. The held-out correlation of each rule with the activations separates them: if “is a formula” scores much higher than “is a technical noun” across all tokens, A; if the broader rule scores higher, B. The top-20 view cannot tell, because both hypotheses predict it.

Practice

Run the quantile view on two neurons the screening in lesson 04 will pick: L0/908 and L9/788. Before running, write what rule you expect from the first band alone; then check the lower bands.

python labs/reference/maxact.py --layer 0 --neuron 908
python labs/reference/maxact.py --layer 9 --neuron 788
What to look for

L0/908 is the digit neuron and L9/788 the punctuation neuron from lesson 04’s table. Layer-0 neurons tend to be token-identity units whose bands agree; deeper ones mix context in. Whatever you see, the question for lesson 04 is the same: what does the rule score on tokens you did not look at?
