Module 1 · Evidence that misleads and evidence that counts

Scoring an explanation by held-out activation prediction

Turn an explanation into predicted activations, score the predictions against the real ones on held-out tokens, and read the number for what it is.

The explain–simulate–score loop

Bills et al. (2023) made the idea operational at scale. A language model writes an explanation of a neuron from its top-activating excerpts; a second model, given only the explanation, simulates the neuron’s activation on each token of new excerpts; the simulation is scored against the truth. They call the result an “explanation score”: “a measure of a language model's ability to compress and reconstruct neuron activations using natural language” [Bills et al. 2023]. The score is a correlation: “If ρ is the correlation coefficient between the true and simulated activations, then we scale simulations so their mean matches that of the true activations, and their standard deviation is ρ times the standard deviation of the true activations. This maximizes explained variance at ρ².”

You do not need a language model to run this loop. The course’s cheap deterministic variant replaces the simulator with a rule: a regex or a predicate on the token and its predecessor that outputs 1 where the explanation says the neuron fires and 0 elsewhere. The score is the Pearson correlation between that 0/1 series and the activations on held-out tokens. It is cruder than a simulator that predicts magnitudes, and it is completely reproducible, which is what you want while learning.

r = Σ(aᵢ − ā)(pᵢ − p̄) / √[Σ(aᵢ − ā)² · Σ(pᵢ − p̄)²]

Here aᵢ is the neuron’s activation on held-out token i, pᵢ is the rule’s prediction (0 or 1), and the bars are means over the held-out tokens. r is 1 if the neuron fires exactly where the rule says and nowhere else, with constant strength; it is 0 if the rule and the neuron are unrelated.

The run

labs/reference/neuron_score.py documents its procedure in its docstring and prints it. The corpus is tokenised and cut into 128-token chunks; even chunks are the screening split, odd chunks are held out. For the rule “token is a digit,” the script searches every neuron in every layer (12 × 3,072 = 36,864 candidates) for the best correlation on the screening split, then scores the winner once on the held-out split beside two controls.

corpus: 3270 tokens -> 13 screening chunks, 12 held-out chunks of 128
pre-registered: held-out r >= 0.5 and above the 95th percentile of 200 random same-layer neurons and of 500 label shuffles

         rule layer neuron  train r held-out r  rand95  shuf95 |rand|max  base rate  verdict
        digit     0    908    0.979      0.978   0.116   0.040     0.334      0.029  demonstrated

Read it column by column. The screening correlation (0.979) is the maximum over 36,864 searches and is not evidence by itself. The held-out correlation (0.978) is the evidence; it barely dropped, which tells you the rule generalises within this corpus. The random-neuron 95th percentile (0.116) says that a typical other neuron in layer 0 scores around 0.1 against the digit rule, and the largest of 200 random neurons scored 0.334; the shuffled-label 95th percentile (0.040) says a meaningless rule on this neuron scores near zero. The base rate, 2.9% of held-out tokens are digits, bounds how much any 0/1 rule can explain of a sparse unit. The verdict applies the pre-registered rule: r ≥ 0.5 and above both control percentiles.

Which score, on which data

The number depends on the excerpts it is computed on, and Bills et al. are candid about this. Their headline figure, “over 1,000 neurons with explanations that scored at least 0.8,” is qualified in the same sentence: “meaning that according to GPT-4 they account for most of the neuron's top-activating behavior.” Their scoring used a “top-and-random” mixture, which “can be thought of as an explanation’s ability to capture the neuron’s most strongly represented feature (from the top text excerpts), with a penalty for overly broad explanations (from the random text excerpts)” [Bills et al. 2023]. Huang et al. (2023) showed what that mixture lets through: “The GPT-4 score is computed on a set of 10 examples from the GPT-2 XL training corpus, 5 containing tokens with top activations and 5 randomly sampled,” and they construct an unfaithful explanation that scores perfectly with high probability. Auditing 300 of the explanations with score at least 0.8, they found “a mean F1 score of 0.56 (with a precision of 0.64 and a recall of 0.50), whereas the random baseline has a F1 score of zero” [Huang et al. 2023].

The lesson is not that correlation is a bad score. It is that the data the score is computed on is part of the claim. The course scores on every token of the held-out split, which is the “random-only” regime and the stricter one, and reports the base rate so you can see how sparse the target is. Anthropic’s dictionary-learning paper notes the same choice: they sampled “across the full range of activation intervals” and distinguish this from Bills et al.’s top-and-random mixture [Towards Monosemanticity].

The lab

labs/explanation_score.py asks for split_chunks, rule_predictions (regex or predicate), score_explanation, the two controls, and a verdict. The tests build a synthetic activation matrix where unit 0 fires on digits and unit 1 on capitalised words, and check that the true rule is demonstrated, that the capital rule applied to the digit unit is not, and that the random-unit control catches the case where the rule is real but belongs to a different unit.

In interpretability work

Report four numbers with every explanation: held-out score, random-unit control percentile, shuffled-label control percentile, base rate. A score without its controls is a story; a score without its base rate is unreadable.

Pitfall

Scoring on the screening split and calling it validation. The screening correlation is the best of thousands; the drop to held-out was small here because the digit rule is nearly exact, but for a weaker rule the drop is the whole story. The eighth row of lesson 05’s table shows one that fell from 0.651 to 0.528.

Check your understanding

An explanation scores r = 0.9 on top-and-random excerpts and r = 0.3 on random-only excerpts. What does the neuron probably look like?

Reveal the reasoning

It probably has a clean strong tail (the top excerpts are captured) and a broad or differently-shaped weak region that the explanation does not predict: on random text the explanation either fires where the neuron is quiet or misses the many weak firings. Bills et al. report this pattern directly: correlation scores of 0.9 still gave average ablation scores of 0.3 on random-only text. Whether the weak region matters is the Gao–Cammarata disagreement; that it exists is what the two scores tell you.

Practice

Run the digit scoring, then implement labs/explanation_score.py until its tests pass. Then write a rule of your own for L2/2928 that is stricter than “after a full stop or newline” (for example, excluding tokens after a closing quote) and score it with the reference script’s machinery.

python labs/reference/neuron_score.py --rule digit
python -m unittest discover -s labs -p 'test_explanation_score.py' -v
What to look for

A stricter rule can raise precision and lower recall at once; whether r rises depends on which error was larger. Record both rules’ held-out r and both controls in your protocol notes. The point of the exercise is to feel the score move for a reason you can name.
