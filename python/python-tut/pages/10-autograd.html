Autograd: what a gradient is operationally, backward(), and a tiny fit

You will rarely train anything in interpretability work, but you will read gradients, attributions, and optimisers constantly. Here is the mechanism in forty lines.

A gradient, operationally

For a scalar loss and a parameter w, the gradient dloss/dw is how much the loss changes per small change in w. The script below checks that definition directly: it nudges w by 1e-3, recomputes the loss, and divides the change by the nudge. The finite difference and the autograd value agree to three decimals. That is what backward() computes, for every parameter at once, without the nudging.

$ python3 labs/examples/autograd_fit.py
at w=0, b=0: loss 7.061, dloss/dw -3.028, dloss/db -5.005
negative gradient says: increase w and b
(loss(w+eps) - loss(w)) / eps = -3.028  (close to dloss/dw)
step   0  loss 7.0606  w 1.514  b 2.502
step  50  loss 0.0031  w 2.976  b 1.016
step 100  loss 0.0028  w 3.027  b 0.989
step 150  loss 0.0028  w 3.029  b 0.988
step 200  loss 0.0028  w 3.029  b 0.988
fit: y = 3.03 x + 0.99   (true 3.00 x + 1.00)
two backward() calls without clearing: grad = tensor([4., 4., 4.]) (accumulated, not replaced)

The mechanism

A tensor with requires_grad=True is a leaf. An operation is recorded in the backward graph only if at least one of its inputs requires grad [PyTorch: autograd]; the result carries a grad_fn that points back at the inputs. loss.backward() walks that graph from the loss to the leaves, applying the chain rule, and accumulates the result into each leaf's .grad. Only leaf tensors with requires_grad=True get a .grad field filled [PyTorch: autograd]; intermediates do not keep theirs. The first line of output is the gradient at w = 0, b = 0: both negative, meaning increasing either parameter would decrease the loss, which is why the update subtracts the gradient.

The training loop, line by line

w.grad = None clears the previous gradient. The last line of the output shows what happens without that: two backward() calls accumulate to 4, not 2, because .grad is summed into, not replaced. Every optimiser's zero_grad() exists for this reason. Then the forward pass builds a fresh graph, backward() fills .grad, and the update w -= lr * w.grad runs inside torch.no_grad() because the update must not itself be recorded; if it were, the next backward would try to differentiate through the optimiser step, and in-place modification of a leaf that requires grad is an error outside no-grad mode. After 200 steps the line is 3.03x + 0.99 against a truth of 3x + 1 plus noise.

That loop is torch.optim.SGD with the optimiser inlined. Real code replaces the three middle lines with opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(), and the mechanism is the same. When you read a training script you are reading this loop plus batching.

Where interpretability meets autograd

Attribution methods differentiate a logit with respect to an activation: logit.backward() after enabling grad on a cached residual-stream tensor gives "how much would this logit move per unit change in each of the 768 features here". Integrated gradients, attribution patching, and gradient-based feature visualisation all rest on exactly the mechanism in this script, with the model's own parameters frozen (requires_grad=False) and the activation or input made a leaf. The interpretability course uses it; this lesson is so that nothing in it will look like magic.

The flip side is the cost. A forward pass that records a graph keeps every intermediate alive for the backward pass. For GPT-2 small that is tens of megabytes per short prompt, which you pay for nothing when you only wanted activations. torch.no_grad() for inference, requires_grad_(True) on the one tensor you want gradients for, and detach() on the way out: the three habits from lesson 09, now with the reason.

Two sanity checks to keep

The finite-difference check in the script is cheap and general: if an attribution number looks odd, nudge the input and see whether the loss moves the way the gradient said. And the shape check: a gradient has the shape of the thing it is with respect to. acts.grad for a (1, 5, 768) activation is (1, 5, 768). If you ever see a gradient of a different shape you have differentiated with respect to something else.

In eval and interpretability work

"Attribution" is a gradient times something. When a paper reports which tokens or features mattered for a prediction, ask what scalar was differentiated, with respect to which tensor, and whether the parameters were frozen. Those three answers are three lines of code, and you can now read them.

Pitfall

Calling backward() on a non-scalar. Autograd needs a scalar to start from; logits.backward() raises, and the fix is to choose the scalar you mean, such as logits[0, -1, token_id], rather than .sum(), which answers a different question.

Check your understanding

The script's gradient at the start is about -3.0 for w and -5.0 for b. Why is the gradient for b larger in magnitude when the true slope is larger than the true intercept?

Reveal the reasoning

The gradient measures sensitivity of the loss, not the size of the parameter. With x in [0, 1], the mean of x is 0.5, so a unit change in w shifts predictions by 0.5 on average while a unit change in b shifts them by 1. The loss is therefore about twice as sensitive to b, and the ratio is close to 5:3 rather than 2:1 because the residuals are not uniform. Sensitivity, not value.

What to look for

Without clearing, gradients accumulate across steps, the effective learning rate grows every step, and the fit diverges or oscillates. Without no_grad, torch raises because an in-place operation on a leaf that requires grad is not allowed to be recorded; the error message says so. Both are the two most common bugs in hand-written training loops.
