Module 4 · Evaluating Agents

Cost frontiers and time horizons

Two research programs close out the agents module by fixing the units we measure in. Princeton's "AI Agents That Matter" argues accuracy without cost is meaningless for agents; METR's time-horizon work converts benchmark scores into a unit a human can actually picture. Both are arguments about metric design.

Accuracy alone cannot identify progress

The Princeton critique starts from an uncomfortable arithmetic fact: agent accuracy "can be improved by scientifically meaningless methods such as retrying." Their illustration: AlphaCode's accuracy climbs from near 0% zero-shot to over 15% with 1,000 retries and over 30% with a million retries.[Agents That Matter] An axis you can buy your way up isn't measuring intelligence; it's measuring budget. Their fix: cost-controlled evaluation — report accuracy jointly with dollar cost, as a Pareto frontier, and compare agents only against that frontier.

The empirical centerpiece re-evaluated three state-of-the-art HumanEval agents — LDB, LATS, and Reflexion — against three deliberately boring baselines (retry up to five times; "warming," retries with gradually rising temperature; and model escalation, trying cheaper models first). The verified result: "There is no significant accuracy difference between our warming strategy and the best-performing agent architecture" — while "the cost can differ by almost two orders of magnitude." Reflexion and LDB cost over 50% more than warming; LATS over 50 times more.[Agents That Matter] Note the precise claim: the baselines match the complex agents' accuracy at a fraction of the cost — a Pareto improvement, not an accuracy win. The deeper implication: because agent papers hadn't tested simple baselines, "the question of whether debugging, reflection, and other such 'System 2' approaches are useful for code generation remains open."[Agents That Matter] Without a cost axis and a baseline, an entire research narrative had gone unchecked.

Who needs the cost axis — and who doesn't

The paper draws a clean line the field had been blurring: "Model evaluation is a scientific question of interest to researchers" — there, abstracting away dollar costs makes sense. "Downstream evaluation is an engineering question that helps inform a procurement decision … Here, cost is the actual construct of interest."[Agents That Matter] Their case study of how proxies mislead: NovelQA, by asking all questions about a novel at once, made retrieval look only 2× cheaper than long-context processing when the realistic sequential workload makes it 20× cheaper — "a tenfold overestimate" of RAG's relative cost.[Agents That Matter] Their practical recommendation: publish input/output token counts alongside dollars, so costs can be recomputed as prices change.

Cost corrupts measurement a second way: it prices rigor out. With a SWE-bench run costing potentially over $8,000 (at the SWE-Agent authors' $4-per-task cap across 2,000+ tasks), almost nobody runs replicates — and when the Princeton team ran five, they "found that many reported accuracy scores were above the maximum of five runs that we performed in our reproduction attempts."[Agents That Matter] Expensive evals get single runs; single runs get cherry-picked; and the leaderboard inherits the bias. (Module 5 makes the statistical case; the same paper's holdout framework — four levels of intended generality, each demanding a different kind of held-out set, with only 5 of 17 agent benchmarks passing[Agents That Matter] — already appeared on page 13.)

METR's time horizon: a unit humans can picture

Benchmark scores don't compose into a picture of how capable agents are getting — 14% on WebArena, 61% on tau-bench retail, 33% on SWE-bench Verified are three incommensurable numbers. METR's contribution is a metric with interpretable units: the 50%-task-completion time horizon — "the time humans typically take to complete tasks that AI models can complete with 50% success rate."[METR] Note the direction: it's a human duration, indexed by model success — "the model has a 2-hour horizon" means it succeeds half the time on tasks that take skilled humans two hours, not that the model runs for two hours.

The methodology is a model of doing measurement properly, and expensively: a 170-task suite spanning 1-second micro-tasks to 8-hour research engineering; 800+ human baseline runs totaling 2,529 hours to price each task in human time (geometric mean of successful baselines); then a logistic regression relating an agent's success probability to log task duration, whose midpoint is the agent's horizon — explicitly modeled on psychometrics' item response theory, with human time standing in for learned difficulty. Fit quality: R² ≈ 0.80 regressing model success against log human time.[METR]

The headline finding (v3, February 2026): the 50% horizon has doubled every ~207 days (95% bootstrapped CI 166–240 days) from 2019 through early 2025 — GPT-2 at 2 seconds, o3 at around 110 minutes — with the most recent models sitting above the long-run trend (o3: p = 0.006), hinting at acceleration.[METR] Naive extrapolation reaches a one-month horizon between mid-2028 and mid-2031 — a projection the authors immediately flag as hostage to external validity and trend changes.[METR]

The 50% vs 80% finding — this module in one statistic

Re-fit the same data demanding 80% success instead of 50%, and the horizons come out 4–6× shorter, while growing at the same doubling rate (204 vs 207 days). The authors' reading: "even models that sometimes succeed on difficult and diverse tasks cannot reliably perform tasks of moderate length."[METR] That is the pass^k lesson in a different coordinate system — and proof that the reliability threshold in your metric is a design choice that changes the answer several-fold. Anyone quoting a time horizon without its success threshold is quoting half a number.

Caveats the authors put on their own metric

The horizon is relative to a task distribution (software and research engineering — not "all work") and to a reference human population: on internal pull-request tasks, external contractors took 5–18× longer than the repository's own maintainers, which shifts the metric by the same factor.[METR] And version your citations: the March 2025 release headlined Claude 3.7 Sonnet at ~50 minutes; the February 2026 v3 headlines o3 at ~110 — same paper, different snapshots. Date-stamp or mislead.

What Module 4 adds up to

Reading an agentic eval now means asking, beyond the harness questions: outcome or process? Real or simulated environment, and who verified its graders? What's the human baseline, measured how? Reliability at what threshold — pass^1 or pass^8, 50% or 80%? At what cost, against what baseline, on whose holdout? Every one of those is a place a headline number can quietly mean less than it appears — and every one is checkable from public artifacts, as this module's papers demonstrated. The remaining threat is deeper: what if the data itself is compromised, or the score measures the wrong construct entirely? That's Module 6 — after Module 5 arms you with the statistics.
