Reliability: pass^k and repeated trials

"Succeeds 61% of the time" sounds like a property of an agent. It isn't — it's an average that hides the question a deployer actually asks: if I give it the same kind of task tomorrow, will it work again? This page covers tau-bench and its metric pass^k, the cleanest tool yet for measuring dependability rather than luck.

tau-bench in brief

tau-bench (Yao, Shinn, Razavi & Narasimhan, at Sierra) evaluates customer-service agents the way page 11 previewed: the agent has database API tools and a policy document; a simulated user (GPT-4-played) converses with it; success requires both that "the final database is identical to the unique ground truth outcome database" and that the agent's replies contained the required information.[tau-bench] Two domains: retail (115 tasks) and airline (50 tasks), each task annotated until only one correct final state is possible — objectivity bought with annotation labor, capping the benchmark at 165 tasks.[tau-bench] Baseline results: the best agent (gpt-4o with function calling) reached 61.2% on retail and just 35.2% on airline.[tau-bench]

Two metrics from the same trials

Run each task n times and count c successes. Two opposite questions can be answered from that same data:[tau-bench]

pass@k — the chance that at least one of k trials succeeds. The discovery metric: right when you can verify candidates and keep the winner (code with tests). Estimator: 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k).

pass^k ("pass hat k") — the chance that all k trials succeed. The reliability metric: right when every attempt faces a real user and there is no retry oracle. Estimator: C(c, k) / C(n, k). The two coincide at k = 1.

A worked miniature shows how far they diverge. Suppose one task is run n = 8 times with c = 6 successes:

pass@2 = 1 − C(2,2)/C(8,2) = 1 − 1/28 ≈ 0.96  pass^2 = C(6,2)/C(8,2) = 15/28 ≈ 0.54

Same agent, same trials: "96% with a retry" or "54% twice in a row." Neither number is more true — they answer different questions, and quoting one where the other belongs is how agents get deployed on false expectations. (The combinatorics, including why these estimators are unbiased, is Appendix D.)

The headline: capable on average, unreliable in series

tau-bench's signature finding: gpt-4o's 61.2% average on retail collapses to below 25% at pass^8 — fewer than a quarter of tasks are solved dependably eight-for-eight, even though the tasks and database transitions are identical across trials; the only randomness is the sampling of the agent's and simulated user's messages.[tau-bench] The authors' framing: "it is important and challenging not just to build agents with high average success (pass^1), but with more robustness and consistency (pass^k trend)."[tau-bench]

Why the decay is steep — and what its shape tells you

If every task had the same success probability p = 0.612, consistency would decay like pk: 0.6128 ≈ 2%. The observed pass^8 (~25%) is far above that, which itself is informative: tasks are heterogeneous — some the agent solves nearly always, others nearly never — so the population splits into "reliable" and "coin-flip" tasks rather than uniform mediocrity. The pass^k curve, not any single point, is the fingerprint of that split. The same data shows it directly: tasks requiring more database writes fail more.[tau-bench]

Probing what the score measures

tau-bench's authors ran the kind of follow-up experiment this course keeps advocating: ablate an input and see if the score cares. Removing the policy document — the rules the agent is supposedly following — cost gpt-4o only 4.4 points on retail (61.2 → 56.8) but 22.4 points on airline (33.2 → 10.8). Their conclusion: retail successes "mostly stem from using tools in an intuitive and common sense way," not from reading the policy.[tau-bench] The retail score, in other words, partly measures something other than what the benchmark was built to measure — found with one cheap ablation. (Construct validity, formally, in Module 6.)

Their failure analysis models another habit worth copying: of 40 failed retail trajectories examined by hand, 4 turned out to be benchmark bugs (instruction typos and ambiguity, subsequently fixed) — and the remaining 36 split into wrong arguments to tools (33.3%), wrong decisions against the rules (25.0%), wrong information given (22.2%), and partially resolved compound requests (19.4%).[tau-bench] Even purpose-built benchmarks ship bugs; error analysis (page 4) applies to the eval itself.

Caveats the authors themselves flag

The reward is "necessary but not sufficient" — a policy-violating run can still score 1 (page 11). The simulated user is GPT-4 with its own quirks, and its instructions were tuned while testing against a GPT-4-based agent — a subtle coupling between instrument and measured system. And the oft-quoted "<50%" headline is the domain-averaged figure (48.2); retail alone is 61.2.[tau-bench]

Using pass^k on your own system

  • Decide which metric your product implies. Retryable-with-verification → pass@k. Every-attempt-counts (user-facing, irreversible actions) → pass^k. Most deployed agents are the second kind; most published numbers are the first.
  • Budget n > k trials per task and use the unbiased estimators rather than raw "ran it k times" frequencies — the raw version is noisy and wasteful (Appendix D).
  • Report the curve. pass^1 through pass^8 tells a deployer what one number can't: how much of your average is dependable skill versus coin-flips.
  • Mind the cost. Repeated trials multiply eval spend — a full tau-bench retail trial ran roughly $0.61 per task in 2024 prices[tau-bench] — which is exactly the tension the next page takes up.
Key idea

Average success and dependable success are different quantities, and the gap between them — the pass^k decay curve — is itself a measurement of your agent's character: skill that repeats, or variance that occasionally lands. For anything user-facing, reliability is the product; measure it as one.
