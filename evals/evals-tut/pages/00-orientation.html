Module 0 · Orientation

Why evals are the job

Building with LLMs feels easy at first — a demo comes together in an afternoon. Then the system meets real users, and the question that decides everything is one the demo never answered: how do you know it works? This page explains why evaluation is the actual bottleneck of AI engineering, and lays out the map this course follows.

The plateau every AI product hits

Hamel Husain, who has consulted on dozens of AI products, reports that the unsuccessful ones almost always share one root cause: "a failure to create robust evaluation systems."[Husain, Evals] The pattern is consistent enough to be a syndrome. His canonical case study is Lucy, a real-estate AI assistant built by the company Rechat: early progress came fast through prompt tweaking, then performance plateaued as the product's surface area grew. The symptoms he lists will sound familiar to anyone who has shipped an LLM feature:

  • Fixing one failure caused others to appear — "resembling a game of whack-a-mole."
  • Nobody could say how well the system actually performed across tasks, "beyond vibe checks."
  • Prompts bloated into unwieldy walls of edge cases and examples.[Husain, Evals]

The way out was not a better prompt. It was building the machinery to measure: logging every interaction as a trace, reading those traces systematically, writing hundreds of small automated checks, and validating any automated grader against human judgment. Husain's framing is that success with AI, like success in software generally, "hinges on how fast you can iterate" — and you cannot iterate on what you cannot measure.[Husain, Evals]

Key idea

An eval is not a report card you fill out at the end. It is the steering mechanism of the development loop: it tells you what's broken, whether your fix worked, and whether the fix broke something else. Teams that treat evaluation as an afterthought don't iterate more slowly — past a certain complexity, they stop being able to iterate at all.

How much of the job is this, really? Husain and Shreya Shankar, whose process material forms the spine of Modules 1–3, put a number on it from their own project work: "we've spent 60–80% of our development time on error analysis and evaluation."[Evals FAQ] That is not a typo, and it is the single most useful expectation to recalibrate before you start. Evaluation isn't a tax on the real work. For LLM systems, it largely is the real work.

Why this is harder than testing normal software

Ordinary software is deterministic: given the same input, it does the same thing, so a test suite of exact assertions can pin its behavior down. An LLM-based system breaks the assumptions that testing tradition rests on, in three stacked ways:

  • The output space is open. There are countless acceptable answers to "summarize this email" and countless unacceptable ones, and no string comparison separates them.
  • The behavior is probabilistic. The same input can produce different outputs across runs, so any score you measure is partly luck — which is why this course has an entire statistics module (Module 5).
  • The failure surface is unbounded. Users will phrase things you never imagined, and the system fails in ways you didn't know existed until you look. You cannot enumerate the test cases up front; you have to discover them from data (Module 2).

Shankar and Husain's book organizes these difficulties as the "Three Gulfs of LLM Application Development" — three gaps that evaluation work has to bridge: the gulf of comprehension (between the developer and the data), the gulf of specification (between the developer and the LLM pipeline), and the gulf of generalization (between the data and the pipeline).[Evals Book] You don't fully know what your users ask, you can't fully specify what you want, and what works on the cases you've seen won't automatically work on the cases you haven't. Every technique in this course is an attack on one of those three gaps.

The map: three layers of evaluation

"Evals" means different things in different rooms, and a lot of confusion comes from not noticing which layer is being discussed. This course covers all three:

1. Product evals — does my application work for my users? Specific to one product, built from that product's real failures, graded against that product's definition of quality. This is the Husain/Shankar territory: error analysis, custom graders, judges aligned with a domain expert. Modules 1–3.

2. Capability benchmarks — how good is the model? Standardized, public, model-versus-model comparisons: SWE-bench, GAIA, MMLU and kin. These are shared scientific instruments — and like any instrument they can be miscalibrated, gamed, or contaminated. Modules 4 and 6 teach you to read them critically.

3. The layer in between — can this system reliably achieve outcomes in my domain? Evaluating agentic systems on outcomes — with the reliability, cost, and validity questions that neither product-eval practice nor public benchmarks fully answer yet. The field's open frontier; Modules 4–6 build the tools for it.

The distinction matters because the methods don't transfer blindly. A capability benchmark score tells you surprisingly little about whether a model will work in your pipeline; a perfect product eval tells you nothing about models you haven't plugged in. Knowing which question you're asking — and which layer answers it — is half of eval literacy.

The second half: distrusting measurements properly

The deeper theme of this course is that the measurement itself can be broken, and usually nothing looks wrong when it is. A few verified examples of what's coming:

  • When LLMs are used to grade other LLMs' outputs, they exhibit systematic biases — most judges favor whichever answer appears first in the comparison, among other documented distortions.[MT-Bench] (Module 3)
  • A famous benchmark's test questions can leak into a model's training data, inflating scores in ways that evaporate on fresh questions of equal difficulty. (Module 6)
  • Two models can differ by several points on an eval and be statistically indistinguishable — noise wearing the costume of progress. (Module 5)
Pitfall

The most common eval failure isn't having no evals — it's having evals you trust more than you should. An unvalidated grader, an underpowered comparison, or a contaminated benchmark is worse than no measurement, because it manufactures confidence. A recurring rule in this course: every measuring instrument must itself be measured before you lean on it.

How to read this course

The modules build in order, but every page stands alone wiki-style — follow cross-links backward when you hit something unfamiliar. Dashed-underlined terms like benchmark show a tooltip on hover and link into the glossary. Statistics appear gently in context, with Appendices A–F teaching the underlying math from zero whenever you want the real machinery.

Every empirical claim here was verified against its primary source — fetched and checked, not recalled — and carries an inline citation; each page ends with a source box, and the annotated bibliography collects the whole citation universe. Where a source's own evidence is weaker than its reputation (it happens more than you'd hope), the page says so. A course on measurement that didn't audit its own claims would be a joke at its own expense.
