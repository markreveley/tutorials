LLM-as-judge: mechanics

Using a model to grade model outputs is the only way to apply human-like judgment at machine scale — and it works well enough to be everywhere. This page covers the machinery: the three judging formats, the evidence that judges can track human preferences, and the prompt-design decisions that determine whether yours will.

The result that legitimized the technique

The canonical citation is Zheng et al.'s MT-Bench paper (NeurIPS 2023), which tested LLM judges against two pools of human judgment: 58 expert-level labelers grading model answers to 80 multi-turn questions, and ~30,000 crowdsourced votes from the Chatbot Arena platform.[MT-Bench] The headline: GPT-4's agreement with humans reached 85%, while humans agreed with each other only 81% of the time.[MT-Bench] A judge that agrees with people as often as people agree with each other is, by that yardstick, a usable instrument.

Caveat: read the fine print on that 85%

Both numbers come from the paper's "S2" setup — only non-tie votes counted, where random guessing scores 50%. Under the stricter S1 setup (ties included, random baseline 33%), the same data gives 66% judge–human and 63% human–human agreement.[MT-Bench] Neither number is wrong — but quoting "85% agreement" without its baseline is exactly the kind of measurement-definition sleight this course keeps warning about. Also: agreement isn't uniform. It climbs from ~70% toward ~100% as the quality gap between compared models widens[MT-Bench] — judges are most reliable on exactly the comparisons you least need help with.

The three judging formats

The MT-Bench paper distinguishes three ways to pose the grading question, each with a verified trade-off profile:[MT-Bench]

FormatThe judge seesStrengthWeakness (paper's own words)
Pairwise comparisonOne prompt, two answers: which is better?More stable; relative judgments are easier"Lack[s] scalability when the number of players increases, given that the number of possible pairs grows quadratically"
Single-answer gradingOne prompt, one answer: score itScales linearly; no pairing needed"May be unable to discern subtle differences"; absolute scores "fluctuate more than relative pairwise results if the judge model changes"
Reference-guided gradingPrompt, answer, and a reference solutionDramatically better on math/objective tasksNeeds a reference — someone must produce it

Encouragingly, the scalable option holds up: in the paper's data, single-answer grading matched pairwise comparison's agreement with humans (85% on MT-bench, S2) — so for production use you aren't forced into quadratic pair grading.[MT-Bench] Eugene Yan's practitioner synthesis adds the complementary rule of thumb: pairwise is "typically used—and more reliable—for subjective evals," producing "more stable results and smaller differences between LLM judgments and human annotations."[Yan] Match the format to the question: pairwise for "which prompt is better," single-answer binary for "does this output pass," reference-guided when a trustworthy answer key exists.

Designing the judge prompt

A judge is a prompt, and the design decisions are few enough to enumerate. The verified guidance:

  • Make the output binary, with a critique. The case for binary judgments was made on page 5; for judges specifically, Yan adds that binary outputs "improve model performance while making it easier to apply classification metrics."[Yan] Scope each judge to one failure mode as a binary classification task[Evals FAQ] — five small judges beat one omnibus rubric.
  • Ask for reasoning before the verdict. Chain-of-thought prompting "improves the accuracy of LLM-evaluators"[Yan] — though not unconditionally: for math grading, MT-Bench found the CoT judge often "makes exactly the same mistake as the given answers," cutting failures only from 14/20 to 6/20, while reference-guided grading cut them to 3/20.[MT-Bench]
  • Use expert critiques as few-shot examples — knowing the cost. Few-shot examples raised GPT-4's position-bias consistency from 65.0% to 77.5% in MT-Bench's tests, but made calls ~4× more expensive and "may introduce new biases," which is why the paper defaulted to zero-shot.[MT-Bench] The few-shot examples that earn their cost are your domain expert's labeled critiques (page 10).
  • Give the judge everything the human grader had. Husain's catalogue of common judge-prompt mistakes is mostly omissions: "Not providing any critiques. Writing extremely terse critiques. Not providing external context… Not providing diverse examples."[Husain, Judge] If the human used customer metadata to judge, a judge without it is graded on a different task.
  • For multi-turn, show the whole conversation. Splitting a two-turn dialogue into separate judge calls made the judge "struggle to locate the assistant's previous response"; presenting both full conversations in one prompt and directing focus to the final turn "significantly alleviate[d]" the problem.[MT-Bench]

Which model judges?

Start strong, economize later: "start with the most capable models available to establish strong alignment with human judgments. You can optimize for cost later once you've established reliable evaluation criteria."[Evals FAQ] A natural worry — can the same model that does the task also judge it? — gets a verified "usually fine" from the same source: the judge is doing a different, narrowly scoped binary task, and what matters is measured alignment with human labels, not model identity.[Evals FAQ] Hold that thought skeptically, though — the next page covers self-preference, the bias that makes same-model judging genuinely risky in comparative settings.

Two more calibration points before you trust any judge prompt. First, sophisticated judge techniques have modest absolute performance: G-Eval, a widely-adopted CoT-plus-form-filling judge method, achieved an average Spearman correlation with human judgments of just 0.514 — "decent," not definitive.[Yan] Second, for narrow recurring checks, a small fine-tuned classifier can beat a prompted frontier judge entirely (page 3). The judge prompt is a hypothesis; page 10 is the experiment.

A minimal judge prompt skeleton (structure, not magic words):

You are evaluating outputs of <system> for ONE criterion:
<criterion, stated concretely, with the expert's definition>.

Context the human grader had: <user metadata, system state>

Examples of expert judgments (critique + verdict):
<few-shot examples drawn from your domain expert's labels>

Now evaluate:
<input> <output>

First write a critique referencing the criterion.
Then output exactly one line: VERDICT: pass | fail
Pitfall

Treating the judge's critique text as evidence the judge is right. Fluent reasoning is what LLMs produce whether or not the verdict is sound — MT-Bench's math-grading failures came with confident explanations that reproduced the wrong answer's error.[MT-Bench] The only evidence a judge works is measured agreement with human labels on a held-out set. Until then you have a plausible-sounding random number generator.
