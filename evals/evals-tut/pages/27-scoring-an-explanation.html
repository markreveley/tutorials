Module 8 · Evaluating explanations

Scoring an explanation

Interpretability research produces sentences like "this neuron fires on legal citations." This page treats such a sentence the way the rest of the course treats a grader: as an instrument whose validity has to be measured on data it never saw. You will learn the simulation-scoring method that made the question quantitative, the numbers it actually produced, and why its own authors warn you not to over-read them.

An explanation is a prediction

A neuron in a language model is a single unit inside one of the model's layers. For every token the model reads, the neuron produces a number, its activation; most neurons produce something near zero most of the time and spike on particular tokens. A neuron explanation is a short natural-language description of when it spikes. The usual way to write one is to pull the texts on which the neuron fired hardest, its max-activating examples, stare at them, and summarize what they have in common.

Written that way, the explanation describes a few dozen examples. Treated as a claim about the neuron, it is a hypothesis that predicts a number for every token of every text: on text the author never looked at, the activation should be high on citation tokens and near zero elsewhere. Until that prediction is checked, the sentence has the standing of an unvalidated grader on page 6: a plausible second opinion, not a measurement.

So, before scoring anything, name the estimand, the one quantity the test is supposed to estimate (the statistics course makes this its first rule). For an explanation it has three parts: which neuron, over what distribution of text, measured by what agreement between predicted and real activations. Change any part and the number means something else; the disputes on the next two pages are mostly about the second and third.

Key idea

An explanation is validated exactly as a grader is: on a held-out set it played no part in producing, against the ground truth it claims to describe. For a grader, ground truth is a human's label; for an explanation, it is the neuron's real activation. Everything else on this page is the mechanics of that one sentence.

The simulation-scoring loop

The method that made this a routine measurement is simulation scoring, introduced by Bills and colleagues at OpenAI in 2023 in "Language models can explain neurons in language models." It has three roles and three steps.[Bills]

Step 1, explain. The explainer model (GPT-4 in the paper) is shown text excerpts with each token's activation beside it, normalized so the neuron's largest activation ever seen is 10 and anything negative is 0, and asked to write one sentence. The excerpts are 64-token windows from the subject model's training data; the ones shown are the top five by activation, drawn from the 20 highest among 50,000.[Bills]

Step 2, simulate. A second prompt gives the simulator the sentence and fresh excerpts and asks the counterfactual: "supposing a proposed explanation accurately and comprehensively explains a neuron's behavior, how would that neuron activate for each token in a particular sequence?"[Bills] The simulator never sees a real activation of the neuron it is simulating, which is what makes this a held-out test rather than a re-description of the examples.

Step 3, score. Line up the simulated activations and the real ones on the same excerpts and compute their correlation:

ρ = Σ (st − s̄)(at − ā) / √( Σ (st − s̄)2 · Σ (at − ā)2 )

where st is the simulated activation on token t, at is the real one, and the sums run over every token in the scoring excerpts. In words: ρ is 1 when the simulated neuron rises and falls exactly with the real one, and "if the explanation has nothing to do with the neuron behavior, the score will tend to be around 0."[Bills] (Correlation coefficients, and when to prefer the rank-based Spearman version, are taught in Appendix E.) Page 28 carries a 20-token example through this formula by hand.

Which excerpts you score on is a design decision with two options in the paper. Random-only scores on random excerpts, "conceptually easy to interpret" as fit to the whole pre-training distribution but high-variance, because most neurons are quiet on most text. Top-and-random, the default, scores on five top-activating excerpts plus five random ones; the random five add "a penalty for overly broad explanations."[Bills] Hold on to that word penalty; the next section is about how small it is.

What the scores were

Applied to all 307,200 MLP neurons of GPT-2 XL, with GPT-4 as both explainer and simulator, the method produced these numbers.[Bills]

The headline the paper leads with, "over 1,000 neurons with explanations that scored at least 0.8," is true, and it is a third of one percent of the neurons.[Bills] The typical neuron's explanation predicts its activations with a correlation of 0.15, scores fall in later layers, and the authors say so: "both GPT-4-based and human contractor explanations still score poorly in absolute terms," and the typical neuron "appeared quite polysemantic," responding to several unrelated things at once.[Bills]

The human comparison sets the ceiling. Non-expert labelers wrote explanations from the same five excerpts and beat GPT-4 "but not by a huge margin. Human performance is also low in absolute terms, suggesting that the main barrier to improved explanations may not simply be explainer model capabilities."[Bills] One sentence about five excerpts is a weak hypothesis about a neuron that does several things, whoever writes it.

Caveat

A score is a property of an explanation, a simulator and a scoring set together. "With a poor simulator, even a very good explanation will get low scores,"[Bills] and Anthropic's version of the method used a different simulator (Claude), Spearman correlation, and scoring examples drawn from across the activation range.[Monosemanticity] A 0.6 from one setup and a 0.6 from another are not the same measurement; report the setup with the score, as page 19 asked for benchmarks.

Why a high score can be cheap

The paper's limitations section is the most useful part of it for an evaluator, because the authors catalogued the ways their own number can be gamed by accident. Four of them recur throughout this module.

The scoring set is nearly blind to false positives. Top-and-random includes the excerpts where the neuron fires, so an explanation that covers those gets credit; but an explanation that is too broad only loses credit if one of the five random excerpts happens to contain the extra thing it wrongly predicts. The authors state the consequence plainly: "an explanation with good recall (high true positive and low false negative rate) will receive a high score, even if it has mediocre precision (i.e. a false positive rate that is high, but not high enough to be measured with five random text excerpts)."[Bills] When they asked GPT-4 to write ten sentences matching each explanation and ran them through the real neuron, roughly 42% of the sentences were false positives and 86% of neurons had at least one, "an independent signal that our explanations are often too inclusive."[Bills] A scoring scheme that measures one error type and not the other is half a validation.

A baseline with no language model in it did better on average. The authors built a lookup table: for each token in the vocabulary, the neuron's mean activation on that token over held-out text, with no context and no explanation. "The token lookup table baseline is much stronger than the token-based linear prediction baseline, substantially outperforming activation-based explanation and simulation on average."[Bills] That is the control check from page 20 applied to explanations: if a dumb baseline scores as well as your sentence, your sentence has not added understanding, it has restated token statistics in English.

The score is correlational. "We currently explain correlations between the network input and the neuron being interpreted on a fixed distribution. Past work has suggested that this may not reflect the causal behavior between the two."[Bills] They also built a more expensive ablation score: replace the real neuron's activations with the simulated ones inside the running model and measure how far the model's output distribution moves. Correlation scores of 0.9 still produced ablation scores of only 0.3 (random-only) and 0.6 (top-and-random) on average.[Bills] Predicting a neuron's number is not the same as predicting what the number does.

The simulator may read cues a human would not. The scheme assumes the simulator reads the sentence as an idealized human would; if explainer and simulator share a quirk, the neuron can respond to X, the explanation say Y, and the simulation come out high on X anyway.[Bills] It is the self-preference problem of page 9: judge and judged are the same kind of system.

A year later, with Leo Gao as first author, the same group wrote the first point into the record as a design flaw: the Bills score "disproportionately depends on recall," and for a feature that fires at the end of "don't stop" or "can't stop," "an explanation activating on all instances of 'stop' achieves a high interpretability score," a failure that worsens as features get sparser and more specific.[Gao SAE]

Two modes of test: observational and interventional

Huang, Geiger, D'Oosterlinck, Wu and Potts (Stanford, 2023) took the Bills explanations and asked what a rigorous test of one would look like. Their answer splits the claim "neuron a represents concept E" into two modes.[Huang]

In the observational mode, the explanation is a claim "that a neuron a activates on all and only input strings that refer to a concept picked out by the proposed explanation E." The two halves are two error types: a Type I error when the neuron fails to fire on a string the explanation covers (a precision failure), a Type II error when it fires on a string the explanation does not cover (a recall failure). Testing both needs both kinds of sentence, so they generated phrases inside the concept and collected corpus sentences on which the neuron fires, hand-checking the result.[Huang]

They evaluated 300 explanations sampled from the roughly 1,700 neurons that Bills et al. scored at 0.8 or above, the ones GPT-4 was most confident in. Result: mean precision 0.64, recall 0.50, F1 0.56; pairing the same explanations with random neurons gave an F1 of zero. And the number that should change how you read every simulation score: "The F1-score has a correlation coefficient of −0.1 with the GPT-4 score."[Huang] Among explanations that all scored at least 0.8, the simulation score said nothing about which would survive a precision-and-recall test.

Their explanation of why is worth memorizing. Take an unfaithful explanation "year 2000 and 2001" for a neuron that only fires on "2000." Its precision is 0.50. The Bills scoring set has five random excerpts; if "2001" appears in a fraction n of excerpts, the chance that at least one of the five contains it is 1 − (1 − n)5, roughly 5n. "For any large corpus, n% could be extremely small due to a long tail distribution, which means the GPT-4 score is insensitive to Type I errors."[Huang] Random text is a weak place to find where a hypothesis is wrong; you have to go looking.

In the intervention mode, the explanation is a causal claim: the neuron mediates the concept, so swapping its activation between two inputs should swap the model's behavior on a task that depends on the concept. Across their tasks the ranking was "token-activation correlation baseline ≫ GPT-4 explanation ≈ random baseline," so "if we were using GPT-4 generated explanation to inform us which weights to modify in a model editing task, we would have similar performance as randomly selecting neurons to edit."[Huang] No task was found where a single neuron's intervention changed behavior. The abstract's summary: "even the most confident explanations have high error rates and little to no causal efficacy."[Huang]

Why this is the page-6 argument again

EvalGen's alignment score keeps coverage and the false-failure rate separate so that neither can hide the other. Huang et al.'s observational mode is the same two-sidedness applied to a neuron: recall over the strings the neuron fires on, precision over the strings the explanation names. One correlation on a recall-heavy sample collapses the two, and the −0.1 is the cost.

Pitfall

Scoring the explanation on the examples that produced it. If the excerpts the explainer saw are also in the scoring set, the score measures whether the explainer can paraphrase its own input. Bills et al. simulate on excerpts the explainer did not see; Anthropic's pipeline states that there is "no leak of information about activation patterns, except for the explanation."[Monosemanticity] Find the sentence in a write-up that says which texts were held out. If it is missing, the score is a training-set score, and Module 6 taught you what those are worth.

Key idea

None of this makes simulation scoring useless; it made a vague practice quantitative and scales to every neuron in a model. But it is not a certificate. Ask three questions of any explanation score. Held out from what? (the explainer must not have seen the scoring text). Sampled how? (random text almost never contains the false positives that break a broad explanation). Correlation with what? (the neuron's activation, or the model's behavior when that activation is replaced). Bills et al. answered the first well, the second weakly by their own account, and the third only in an appendix; Huang et al. built the tests for the second and third; the next page adds the controls, and the case study shows a 2026 dispute that turns on all three.
