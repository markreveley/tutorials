Module 5 · Statistics for Evals

Comparing two models

"Model B scored two points higher" is the most common sentence in AI engineering, and most of the time nobody knows whether it means anything. This page covers the two tools that settle it — paired analysis and power analysis — including a worked case where doing the statistics properly reverses the naive verdict.

The question behind the question

Both models' scores are sample estimates with error bars (previous page), so their difference is also an estimate with an error bar, and the real question is whether that difference is distinguishable from zero — a hypothesis test (Appendix C from zero). The naive approach compares the two summary scores as if they were independent measurements. For evals, that throws away the most valuable structural fact you have: both models answered the same questions.

Paired differences: the free lunch

Question difficulty is shared. A brutal question is brutal for both models; a gimme is a gimme for both. So model scores across questions are positively correlated, and that correlation is pure noise for the purpose of comparison — it affects both models identically and cancels if you let it. The paired method: for each question i, compute the per-question difference sA,i − sB,i, and run your inference on those differences.[Error Bars] The variance of the paired estimate shrinks by twice the covariance between the models' scores — in Miller's worked example, a typical correlation of 0.5 cuts estimator variance by a third, which he calls "a 'free' reduction in estimator variance," recommending the paired version "wherever practicable."[Error Bars] Free, because it needs no new model calls — only refusing to discard the pairing you already have.

The worked case: a verdict reversed

Miller's running example deserves retelling in full, because its punchline is the thesis of this module. Two fictional models, Galleon and Dreadnought, are compared on three evals. Read naively — compare the headline scores, no error bars — Dreadnought wins two of three and looks like the better model. The only statistically significant difference favors Galleon — the model the naive reading rejected. Dreadnought's two "wins" came on the small eval (HumanEval, 164 questions) and the clustered one (MGSM), exactly where error bars are widest. Miller: "a closer examination of the data would tend to lead the careful analyst to the opposite conclusion."[Error Bars] The numbers are fictional; the failure mode is not. Model selections, pricing decisions, and launch announcements ride on comparisons exactly this fragile.

Power analysis: can your eval even see the difference you care about?

The second tool runs before you measure. Statistical power asks: if a real difference of size δ exists, what's the probability my eval detects it? Decide the effect size you care about — say, "I'd switch models for 3 points" — and the machinery (Miller's Eq. 9; gentle version in Appendix C) tells you the required sample size. Miller's worked example: detecting a 3-percentage-point difference at the conventional 5% significance / 80% power level requires n ≈ 969 questions under reasonable assumptions — the basis for his rule of thumb that "new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions in order to have good signaling ability."[Error Bars]

The formula inverts usefully when n is fixed: with only 198 questions, the minimum detectable difference is about 13 points — and resampling each question 10 times (page 16's K lever) shrinks it to about 7.5.[Error Bars] Run that arithmetic on your own eval suite once and you'll know whether your weekly "model A vs model B" ritual is measurement or ceremony. An eval that cannot detect the smallest difference you'd act on is underpowered theater — it produces verdicts, but the verdicts are coin flips dressed in decimals.

Why agent evals fail this test constantly

Recall from Module 4: a full SWE-bench run can cost thousands of dollars, so teams run once — n = 1 in the resampling sense, on a few hundred tasks, no error bars. The Princeton team's five-run reproductions found published scores outside the entire range of their five runs.[Agents That Matter] When measurement is expensive, the temptation is to economize on replicates — which converts an expensive measurement into an expensive anecdote. Power analysis at least tells you the price of a real answer before you spend.

Pitfall: the silent multiple comparison

Run enough comparisons and "significant at 95%" loses its meaning — one in twenty noise-only comparisons clears the bar by construction (Type I error). Eval dashboards invite exactly this: twenty metrics, re-checked every run, and the week one of them twitches becomes a finding. Decide your comparisons before looking, and treat any unexpected "significant" twitch as a hypothesis to re-test on fresh data, not a result. (Appendix C covers the p-hacking family of errors.)

Key idea

Two disciplines turn comparisons from vibes into measurements: pair what you can (same questions → analyze per-question differences; it's free precision), and power what you plan (decide the effect you care about, then check your eval can see it). Everything else on a comparison dashboard is decoration around those two moves.
