Module 5 · Statistics for Evals

Reproducibility

Error bars handle the randomness inside one evaluation. This page handles the variation between evaluations — why two teams measuring "the same benchmark" get different numbers, and the practices that make a result something another team can actually reproduce. The spine source is EleutherAI's "Lessons from the Trenches," written by maintainers of the field's most used harness.

The root cause, stated once

EleutherAI's diagnosis of why language model evaluation resists standardization: many syntactically different strings mean the same thing, and "our best automated tools for determining whether two sentences are semantically equivalent are the very models we are seeking to evaluate."[Eleuther] Every evaluation method is therefore a workaround — multiple choice, exact match after normalization, model grading — and every workaround embeds choices that other implementers will make differently. Page 2 showed the magnitudes: prompt style swinging a score by ~46 points and reversing model rankings; three independent MMLU implementations producing "widely different scores" that "even change the ranking order of models."[Eleuther] Here the question is what to do about it.

The failure catalogue, by layer

The paper's catalogue of reproducibility hazards maps neatly onto the harness anatomy:

LayerHazard (verified)
PromptFormat/wording sensitivity "even down to whitespace usage"; which format wins varies by model[Eleuther]
TokenizationTokenizing prompt and target separately then concatenating "is not always valid, and most widely-used language model tokenizers provide no such guarantees"[Eleuther]
Scoring methodLog-likelihood-scored and generatively-scored versions of the same multiple-choice benchmarks "do not even agree"; APIs that hide probabilities force the generative route[Eleuther]
Answer extractionRegex extraction is "a highly imperfect solution" that conflates format compliance with correctness[Eleuther]
AggregationMMLU micro vs macro averaging moves results "by several percentage points," usually unreported[Eleuther]
Dataset itselfQuirks accumulate: an ARC question with five options instead of four; three HumanEval problems missing example tests — which different agent papers then patched in three different ways[Eleuther][Agents That Matter]

Agentic evaluation adds its own layer: the Princeton reproducibility audit found eval scripts that assume particular agent designs, environment statefulness (the WebArena rate-limit ordering effect from page 11), and scoring bugs — two leaderboard agents "marked some incorrectly completed tasks as correct" and quietly removed benchmark tasks (one removed 1 task, the other 8).[Agents That Matter]

When divergence becomes strategy

Implementation sensitivity isn't always an accident to fix; once scores carry stakes, it becomes a surface to exploit. The verified example: on the Open LLM Leaderboard, "practitioners have been observed fine-tuning their models specifically to match the evaluation format used by the leaderboard, such as targeting separator tokens used in particular versions of lm-eval, rather than improving the underlying capabilities being measured."[Eleuther] The harness quirk becomes the optimization target — a small-scale preview of overfitting-to-the-instrument dynamics that Module 6 meets at industrial scale. The paper's blunt summary of where this leaves the field: the popularity of informal "vibes tests" and crowdsourced arenas over benchmarks is "a powerful indictment of the current state of benchmarking."[Eleuther]

The practices that fix it

The paper's best-practices checklist is short enough to adopt verbatim:[Eleuther]

  • "Always share your exact prompts" — and the full eval code; disclose how much prompt engineering was done, and don't tune prompts to favor one model.
  • "Avoid copying results from other implementations" — cross-paper numbers differ by prompts, sample size, and metric calculation; rerun, don't transcribe.
  • "Always provide model outputs" — raw generations let others re-grade without re-running, separating scoring disputes from sampling noise.
  • "Perform qualitative analyses" — read a sample of outputs before trusting a score (Module 2's error-analysis discipline, at benchmark scale).
  • "Perform statistical significance testing" and report variance — Module 5's whole argument.
  • Use a standardized harness (lm-eval or HELM) and report its version — naming the lm-eval task and version "has become a de facto reporting standard."

A standardized harness doesn't make the arbitrary choices go away — it makes them shared and versioned, so a number travels with its exact recipe. lm-eval also operationalizes the statistics: bootstrapped standard errors are reported by default, making error bars "nearly as simple as copy-pasting an additional number into one's paper."[Eleuther] One precision the paper adds and most people miss: bootstrap-over-questions and variance-over-reruns measure different uncertainties — which questions you asked versus which samples you drew — and a careful report distinguishes them.[Eleuther]

Caveat on scope

The paper's authors scope their evidence mostly to multiple-choice-style evaluation ("the approach we have the most experience with") and urge caution generalizing; and the verified text here is the v3 revision (May 2026), which includes material absent from the 2024 original.[Eleuther] Apply the meta-lesson to the source itself: reproducibility claims are also version-stamped.

Key idea

A reproducible eval result is a bundle: number + prompts + code + harness version + raw outputs + uncertainty. Strip any element and what remains isn't a smaller result — it's a rumor with decimals. The checklist above is the minimum bundle, and none of it is expensive; what it costs is admitting the number alone was never the result.

Module 5 gave you the tools for honest uncertainty. Module 6 turns to the harder failure: a score that is precise, reproducible — and measuring the wrong thing.
