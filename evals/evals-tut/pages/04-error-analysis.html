Module 2 · The Eval Process

Error analysis: look at your data

Where do eval criteria come from? Not from brainstorming meetings, and not from a vendor's metric list. They come from reading what your system actually did, failure by failure, until the failures organize themselves into categories. This page teaches that process — the single highest-leverage activity in all of AI engineering.

The strongest claim in this course

The practitioners whose process forms this module's spine — Husain and Shankar, whose material has been refined across cohorts totaling 4,500+ engineers and PMs[Maven] — report spending "60–80% of our development time on error analysis and evaluation."[Evals FAQ] And within that, the bedrock practice is not building dashboards or judges. It's reading traces: the full logged record of each interaction — inputs, intermediate steps, tool calls, outputs. Husain's rules are written like safety placards because teams keep violating them: "You must remove all friction from the process of looking at data," and "You are doing it wrong if you aren't looking at lots of data."[Husain, Evals]

Why does reading raw traces beat clever automation? Recall the "gulf of comprehension" from the orientation: you don't know what your users actually ask or how your system actually fails until you look.[Evals Book] Husain is candid that even his most famous artifact is secondary to this: "creating a LLM judge is a nice 'hack' I use to trick people into carefully looking at their data! … The real business value comes from looking at your data."[Husain, Judge]

The method: open coding, then axial coding

Error analysis borrows its method from qualitative social science, and the borrowed names are worth keeping because they mark two genuinely different activities:

Step 1 — open coding. Read each trace and write a free-form note about what went wrong, in your own words, with no preset categories. One discipline matters here: note the first failure in each trace, because upstream errors cause downstream ones — a bad retrieval step makes every later step look broken.[Evals FAQ]

Step 2 — axial coding. Group the notes into a failure taxonomy: distinct, named categories of failure — then count the traces in each. The FAQ flags this as "the most important step": the counts turn anecdotes into priorities.[Evals FAQ]

A worked miniature. Suppose you open-code eight traces of a recipe-bot: 

#1  "asked for vegan, suggested honey"          → constraint ignored
#2  "asked for a substitution, invented a tool   → hallucinated capability
     the app doesn't have ('scan your pantry')"
#3  "metric units requested, gave cups"          → constraint ignored
#4  "answer correct but buried in 400 words"     → verbosity
#5  "asked about allergens, gave medical advice" → out-of-scope advice
#6  "asked for vegan, suggested fish sauce"      → constraint ignored
#7  "correct, concise"                           → (pass)
#8  "invented a 'meal plan export' feature"      → hallucinated capability

Axial coding collapses eight notes into a taxonomy with counts: dietary constraint ignored (3), hallucinated capability (2), out-of-scope advice (1), verbosity (1). Already, with eight traces, you know your top problem — and it isn't the one the angriest Slack thread was about. Real analyses do this over a hundred-plus traces, but the mechanics are exactly these.

How many traces? Until saturation

The stopping rule is not a fixed number but a concept borrowed from grounded theory: theoretical saturation — you stop when new traces stop teaching you anything. The FAQ's operational version: "aim to review at least 100 traces" to start, and "if ~20 traces don't turn up a new category, you can stop."[Evals FAQ] For teams that can't commit to that yet, the same source's minimum viable version: 30 minutes reviewing 20–50 outputs after every significant change.[Evals FAQ]

Can an LLM do this for me?

Partly — but not first. The FAQ's guidance is to open-code 30–50 traces yourself before letting an LLM take a first pass at categorizing the rest.[Evals FAQ] The reason connects to criteria drift: until you've personally seen the failures, you don't know what categories exist, so you can't check the LLM's categorization — you'd be delegating a judgment you haven't yet formed. The same logic explains why outsourcing error analysis to a labeling vendor is "usually a big mistake."[Evals FAQ]

Tooling: deliberately boring

What tool should you read traces in? The consistent practitioner answer: the one with least friction, even if you build it yourself. Husain renders traces "in domain-specific ways" and has "often found that it's better to build my own data viewing & labeling tool" — his example was built with Shiny for Python in under a day — alongside the blunt advice: "Keep it simple. Don't buy fancy LLM tools. Use what you have first."[Husain, Evals] A spreadsheet that your domain expert will actually open beats a platform they won't. (Module 7 covers tracing infrastructure for when scale forces the issue.)

Error analysis never finishes

The taxonomy you build today describes today's system. Models get swapped, prompts edited, features added — and the failure distribution moves. The FAQ's cadence: re-run error analysis with 100+ fresh traces every review cycle (typically 2–4 weeks), and between major analyses, review 10–20 traces weekly, focusing on outliers.[Evals FAQ] Sampling can get lighter as the system matures — Husain: "you can sample your data more over time, lessening the burden" — but "you can never stop looking at data—no free lunch exists."[Husain, Evals]

Pitfall

Skipping straight to metrics. A team that stands up a dashboard of generic scores without ever reading traces has automated the measurement of failure modes they haven't discovered yet. The dashboard will be green, the users unhappy, and nobody will know why — the Rechat plateau from the orientation, with better graphs. Error analysis is the step that tells you what to measure; everything downstream inherits its quality.

Key idea

Evals are discovered, not designed. The failure taxonomy — categories found in real traces, with counts — is the source from which test datasets, graders, and judges are all derived. The next page performs exactly that derivation.
