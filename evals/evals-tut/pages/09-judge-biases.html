Module 3 · LLM-as-Judge

Judge biases and failure modes

An LLM judge doesn't fail randomly — it fails systematically, in directions that have been measured. This page catalogues the documented biases with their actual numbers, including one famous bias whose evidence is weaker than its reputation, and the mitigations with verified effect sizes.

Position bias: the order of answers changes the verdict

Show a judge two answers and ask which is better; then swap their order and ask again. A consistent judge gives the same verdict. Measured judges mostly don't. In MT-Bench's controlled test, position bias was severe enough that only GPT-4 stayed consistent in more than 60% of cases — 65.0% with the default prompt — while GPT-3.5 managed 46.2% and Claude-v1 just 23.8%.[MT-Bench] "Most LLM judges favor the first position" — though not all: Claude-v1, when answers were renamed, showed a bias toward whatever was labeled "Assistant A," a name bias riding on top of the position bias.[MT-Bench]

The Panickssery–Bowman–Feng self-preference study (next section) independently measured the same phenomenon as preference reversal rates — how often the verdict flips when options swap: GPT-4 25%, GPT-3.5 58%, Llama 2 89%.[Self-Pref] Two different labs, two methodologies, one conclusion: a single-pass pairwise verdict is substantially an artifact of ordering.

Verified mitigations:

  • Swap and require agreement — judge twice with orders swapped; "only declare a win when an answer is preferred in both orders," else call it a tie. This is MT-Bench's own conservative protocol.[MT-Bench]
  • Swap and average — prompt twice and average the confidence scores, the self-preference paper's approach.[Self-Pref]
  • Few-shot calibration — raised GPT-4's consistency from 65.0% to 77.5%, at ~4× the cost.[MT-Bench]

Verbosity bias: longer looks better

Verbosity bias — favoring the longer answer independent of quality — was probed with a designed attack: take a model's answer, have it restated as a "repetitive list" that adds words but no information, and see whether judges fall for it. Failure rates on the 23 attacked answers: Claude-v1 91.3%, GPT-3.5 91.3%, GPT-4 8.7%.[MT-Bench] The calibration detail matters: all three judges correctly tie identical answers, so the bias isn't noise — it's a learned preference for padding. The practical defense is indirect but effective: binary single-answer grading against a concrete criterion gives verbosity nothing to win; verbosity is mostly a pairwise-comparison disease.

Self-enhancement bias: famous, and officially inconclusive

The claim everyone repeats — "judges favor their own outputs" — deserves the careful version, because it's a two-paper story and the first paper explicitly declined to conclude it.

MT-Bench catalogued self-enhancement bias as a potential limitation and reported suggestive numbers: GPT-4 as judge favored its own answers by a 10% higher win rate, Claude-v1 by 25% — but GPT-3.5 did not favor itself, and the authors wrote plainly: "our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias."[MT-Bench] A course on measurement should hold that up as a model of honest reporting — and as a warning about how "suggestive" hardens into "established" through repeated citation.

The stronger evidence came a year later. Panickssery, Bowman and Feng studied self-preference directly on two summarization tasks and added the mechanism: self-recognition. The verified chain of findings:[Self-Pref]

  • GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Llama 2 all "disproportionately favor summaries written by themselves over those by other LLMs and from humans."
  • All three models recognize their own outputs above chance out of the box (pairwise format); GPT-4 hits 73.5% accuracy distinguishing its text from two other LLMs and humans.
  • Fine-tuning GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 on just 500 examples pushes self-recognition above 90% — and self-preference strength rises linearly with self-recognition ability, with control fine-tunes (on length, vowel-counting, readability) falling on the same trend line, ruling out the obvious confounds.
  • The cleanest demonstration: swap the authorship labels, and GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 flip to preferring whichever summary is labeled as their own. The preference attaches to perceived authorship, not text quality.
Caveat — state the causal claim the way the authors do

The paper says its experiments "can only provide evidence towards the causal hypothesis without fully validating it."[Self-Pref] Teach it as a well-controlled intervention that makes the recognition→preference story credible — not proven. Scope notes: news summarization, 2023-era models; weaker models can't even distinguish themselves from stronger ones (Llama 2 vs GPT-4), so the bias profile is model-dependent. And prior papers conflict on which models self-prefer most — the authors use exactly that conflict to argue for multi-dataset evaluation before generalizing.

The practical upshot for the "same model as task and judge" question from page 8: for scoped binary checks against criteria, it remains "usually fine"; for comparative evaluation where the judge's own output is among the candidates — leaderboards, model selection, best-of-n reranking — use a judge that isn't a contestant, or obfuscate authorship, the countermeasure the paper itself proposes.[Self-Pref]

Competence gaps: the judge can't grade what it can't do — and sometimes not even what it can

Bias bends judgments; competence gaps break them. Three verified examples, in increasing order of discomfort:

  • Math grading fails even within competence. GPT-4, shown wrong answers to elementary math problems it can solve when asked directly, "was misled by the provided answers" — judging wrong solutions correct in 14 of 20 trials with the default prompt. Reference-guided grading (judge generates its own solution first, then grades against it) cut failures to 3 of 20.[MT-Bench]
  • Detection is asymmetric. The factual-consistency judge from page 3 caught >95% of consistent summaries but only 30–60% of inconsistent ones[Yan] — judges are systematically better at confirming than at catching.
  • Hallucination detection barely beats chance. Best HaluEval result: 58.5% accuracy.[Yan]

The meta-bias: judges are persuasive

One more MT-Bench finding belongs in this catalogue because it compromises the auditor: when human reviewers disagreed with GPT-4's judgment and were then shown its reasoning, they "deemed GPT-4's judgments reasonable in 75% of cases and are even willing to change their choices in 34% of cases."[MT-Bench] Whether that's the judge being right or being persuasive, the operational lesson is the same: collect human labels before showing humans the judge's output. An alignment measurement where the human saw the judge's critique first is contaminated in the same direction the judge is biased.

Key idea

Every bias in this catalogue was found the same way: controlled perturbation. Swap the order — does the verdict flip? Pad the answer — does the score rise? Swap the authorship labels — does the preference follow? You can run these probes on your own judge in an afternoon, with your own data. A judge you haven't probed isn't unbiased; it's unexamined.

Pitfall

Patching biases one by one and declaring victory. Mitigations interact: few-shot examples reduce position bias but "may introduce new biases"[MT-Bench]; tighter rubrics can shift errors from false passes to false failures. The end-to-end check — measured agreement with fresh human labels, the subject of the next page — is the only metric that catches the bias you didn't think to probe for.
