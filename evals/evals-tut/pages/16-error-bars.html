Module 5 · Statistics for Evals

Error bars on eval scores

An eval score without an error bar is a point pretending to be a fact. This page teaches the statistical frame that makes scores honest — built on Evan Miller's "Adding Error Bars to Evals," the single most practical paper on eval statistics — and the clustering trap that makes even teams who report error bars overconfident.

The reframe: your eval is a sample

The foundational move is conceptual, not mathematical. Miller's framing: "Suppose that the questions in an eval do not represent all possible questions, but instead were drawn at random from a (hypothetical, infinite, unseen) super-population of questions."[Error Bars] What you care about is the model's performance on that population — all the questions you could have asked. What you have is its performance on the few hundred you happened to ask. The gap between those two is sampling error, it is always present, and quantifying it is what error bars do. (If "standard error" and "confidence interval" are new, Appendix B builds them from zero.)

With that frame, the machinery is one line. For per-question scores s with mean s̄ over n questions, the Central Limit Theorem gives the standard error:

SE = √( Var(s) / n )  — for pass/fail scores: SE = √( s̄(1−s̄) / n )

and a 95% confidence interval of s̄ ± 1.96·SE.[Error Bars] Concretely: 140 of 200 questions correct gives 70% with SE ≈ 3.2 points — the honest claim is "somewhere around 63.6% to 76.4%," a 13-point-wide band that most leaderboard gaps would vanish inside. Miller's reporting recommendation is correspondingly plain: publish the question count and the standard error in parentheses next to every score.[Error Bars]

Does anyone actually get this wrong? Yes — in both directions

Miller audits the Llama 3 technical report in passing: it applied the Bernoulli (pass/fail) SE formula to fractional scores like F1, making those intervals too wide — while its reading-comprehension intervals ignored clustering (below) and were likely too narrow.[Error Bars] And EleutherAI's reproducibility work shows what omission costs: GPQA results for frontier models presented as single-run point estimates "paint a very different picture" than the same results with 95% CIs over ten runs — small benchmarks like HumanEval (164 questions) can reverse conclusions under resampling.[Eleuther]

The clustering trap

The CLT formula assumes questions are independent draws. Real benchmarks routinely violate this: reading-comprehension evals ask several questions about each passage; multilingual evals like MGSM translate the same question into many languages. Questions within such a group succeed or fail together — if the model misreads the passage, it misses all five questions about it. Your effective sample size is closer to the number of groups than the number of questions, and the naive SE is too small.

The fix is clustered standard errors, borrowed from social science (mechanics in Appendix F). The verified magnitudes, measured on real Anthropic model results:[Error Bars]

DROP9,622 questions in 588 passages0.441.343.05×

A 3× understatement of your uncertainty means intervals that claim 95% confidence while earning far less. Miller: "Failure to adjust standard errors for clustered sampling may lead an unsuspecting analyst to suppose that the measurement of the overall eval score is much more precise than it actually is."[Error Bars] The agentic parallel from Module 4: tasks instantiated from shared templates (WebArena's 812 tasks from 241 templates) are clusters too.

Shrinking the bars honestly: resample, don't cool

Error bars have two ingredients you can attack separately. Miller decomposes the variance into question difficulty spread (some questions are just harder — a fixed property of the population, irreducible) plus per-question randomness (the same model answering the same question differently across samples — reducible).[Error Bars] The honest lever is resampling: answer each question K times and score the question by its mean. In his worked example, K=2 cuts total variance by a third and K=4 by half, with a hard ceiling of two-thirds — beyond which more samples buy nothing, because only the reducible component shrinks.[Error Bars] (Compute the SE across question-level means; pooling all K·N answers as if independent is inconsistent.) Where the task needs no chain-of-thought, scoring the model's next-token probabilities directly achieves that entire ceiling in one call.[Error Bars]

Pitfall: "don't touch the thermostat"

The tempting shortcut — set temperature to 0 so runs stop varying — gets a dedicated warning section in the paper. Cooling the sampler doesn't remove uncertainty; it can relocate it into the irreducible component and bias the score: in Miller's examples, zero temperature triples the minimum achievable variance in one case and shifts the expected score while increasing difficulty-spread variance roughly five-fold in another.[Error Bars] Evaluate at the temperature you deploy at, and buy precision with K, not with the thermostat.

The five recommendations

Miller's paper closes on exactly five practices — quoted verbatim, since they compress this page and the next into a checklist:[Error Bars]

  1. "Computing standard errors of the mean using the Central Limit Theorem"
  2. "When questions are drawn in related groups, computing clustered standard errors"
  3. "Reducing variance by resampling answers and by analyzing next-token probabilities"
  4. "When two models are being compared, conducting statistical inference on the question-level paired differences, rather than the population-level summary statistics"
  5. "Using power analysis to determine whether an eval (or a random subsample) is capable of testing a hypothesis of interest"

Items 1–3 were this page. Items 4 and 5 — the comparison machinery, where proper statistics can flip a leaderboard verdict outright — are the next page.

Key idea

A score is an estimate; the error bar is the part of the measurement that tells you how much the estimate is allowed to mean. Reporting one without the other isn't simplification — it's omitting the half of the result that says whether the other half could be noise.
