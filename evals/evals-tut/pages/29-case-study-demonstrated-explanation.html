Module 8 · Evaluating explanations

Case study: the demonstrated-explanation standard

In October 2026 a long essay argued that most neurons in large language models can be understood directly, and a short reply argued that an explanation you cannot simulate is not yet an explanation. Both authors worked on the 2023 paper that invented simulation scoring. This page reads their disagreement with the tools from pages 27 and 28, and ends with the checklist a write-up must satisfy before it claims to have explained a neuron.

What was found, and how it was verified

The roadmap behind this module cites an essay by Nick Cammarata on auditing native neurons in Qwen3-32B and a trillion-parameter Kimi model, and a critique by Leo Gao that an explanation which cannot predict a neuron's activations on held-out data has not been demonstrated. Both were found. The essay, "Neural Networks Are Already Clean," is published by Goodfire, calls itself "an informal research essay and working hypothesis," and defers detail to a technical companion paper that had not appeared when this page was written.[Already Clean] Gao's critique is a reply on X to the essay's announcement, dated 15 October 2026, retrieved through two independent mirrors because x.com refused a direct fetch; its full text is quoted below.[Gao reply] The roadmap's sentence is a paraphrase; Gao's own wording is shorter. His reply promises a longer post, which could not be located, and nothing here relies on it.

Caveat

Neither document is peer reviewed, the essay calls its numbers preliminary and threshold-dependent, and the companion paper with the full methods is still to come. This page does not adjudicate who is right about neurons. It uses the exchange as a worked example of evaluating an interpretability claim, which is possible because both sides state their methods and their numbers.

The claim

The essay's thesis is that the canonical evidence for polysemanticity was misread and that "we can now understand most neurons in models up to trillions of parameters."[Already Clean] Its quantitative core is an audit: "In preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B, we estimate that roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job, often with small related offshoots; another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood," based on a gallery of 1,000 "neuron papers, detailed investigations into individual neurons, chosen randomly."[Already Clean] The author sets this against the 2023 baselines he helped produce: Bills et al.'s "about 1.7% of neurons interpretable by our most generous measure" and Anthropic's median neuron rubric score of 0.[Already Clean] (Both figures check out against the primary sources; see pages 27 and 28.)

Read as an evaluator, the first question is the estimand from page 27: 90% of what, measured how? The essay answers candidly. The unit is a "neuron pole" (the positive and negative directions of a neuron, assessed separately), the judgment is whether an agent's investigation found "one main job," and the number moves with "how you set the threshold for counting weak activations, depth, density of sub-circuits, and what you view as 'one thing'"; a single cleanliness score "should be taken as a broad pointer to the regime a neural network is in, rather than something precise to either optimize directly or hold too tightly."[Already Clean] On the trillion-parameter claim, the essay reports "similar results in models up to trillions of parameters, like Kimi K3, which the companion paper will cover in more detail," shows one attribution graph from that model, and adds: "We haven't begun full audits of this scale yet."[Already Clean] The verified claim is narrower than the roadmap's summary: a preliminary, threshold-dependent audit of one 32B model, and an extrapolation to a larger one.

The essay's own prediction test

What makes the essay a useful case rather than a target is that it runs a held-out test of the kind this module demands, under the heading "Explanations That Could Lose": "For 200 random neurons from our gallery, an agent read only each neuron's explanation and wrote five new examples that should drive it and five near misses that shouldn't, before we measured any of them." Of the 183 neurons whose explanation described the positive pole, "the new examples fired their neurons strongly 76% of the time; the near misses, 11%. The explanation won for 164, tied for 15 and lost for 4," where "strong" means at least 30% of the neuron's largest activation in the corpus.[Already Clean] Texts about "the most distant concept" the agent could invent fired strongly about 1% of the time; "179 of the 183 neurons never fired on them at all."[Already Clean]

Run this through page 28's controls. It is held out in the sense that matters: the examples were written from the explanation alone, before measurement, so the explanation could lose. It has a within-neuron contrast (76% against 11%) that works as a precision test on the explanation's boundary. What the fetched text does not report is the control-neuron score: the same new examples scored against random other neurons. The near-miss and distant-concept rates partly stand in for it; the essay says the forthcoming paper "has many more prediction metrics and positive controls" and that "we're mostly not trying to claim a perfect explanation of each neuron here."[Already Clean] On the larger audit, the author calls the attribution-graph reviews "qualitative evidence—I don't trust our current tools enough to turn it into a cleanliness score."[Already Clean]

The essay also takes a position on page 27's method, simulation scoring, in a note titled "On Simulation Scores": simulation "often end[s] up measuring where attention puts information," rewards "fine details of weak activations that matter little for understanding what a neuron does," and asks language models for precise numbers they "usually aren't trained for"; the author would "rather rely on normal science than on any one metric," allowing that "simulation might eventually become one useful score among several."[Already Clean] Elsewhere he describes the 2023 metrics as "rewarding explanations that could simulate activations rather than ones that were right, which often come apart."[Already Clean] The second half of that sentence is a validity claim whose mechanism pages 27 and 28 have already documented: a recall-heavy score can be earned by a broad or single-token explanation.

The critique

Gao's reply, in full:[Gao reply]

"this is cool work, but i think people are overupdating on how optimistic to be about interp.

in particular, we need to more rigorously evaluate explanations, rather than just going off vibes. imo if you can't simulate a neuron you haven't really explained it. my guess is simulating the neurons is way harder than finding plausible explanations. I have a post on this in the works.

(i know the blog talks argues simulations are bad, but i think all of the downsides described are avoided by looking at KL instead of neuron correlation.)"

Three sentences carry the argument. "If you can't simulate a neuron you haven't really explained it" is the demonstrated-explanation standard in its strongest form: a hypothesis that yields no predictions on new data has not been tested, whatever its plausibility. "Simulating the neurons is way harder than finding plausible explanations" is page 28's selection-bias warning as a prior: plausible stories are cheap, because the examples that suggest them were chosen to fit. And the parenthetical concedes the essay's objections to token-level correlation while proposing a different target: put the simulated values into the running model and measure the divergence (KL) of its output from the original. That is the ablation score Bills et al. built and set aside as expensive, and the downstream-divergence metric Gao's own 2024 paper uses; it ignores the fine detail of weak activations exactly to the extent the model does.[Gao SAE]

So the dispute is narrower than the headlines. Both sides want held-out prediction written before the look; the essay's "explanations that could lose" is that. They differ on what must be predicted: whether new sentences built from the explanation fire the neuron strongly (a yes/no on the explanation's own terms), or whether the explanation reproduces the neuron well enough that the model cannot tell the difference, which the near-miss rate does not measure and which the essay's note argues is the wrong first question. In the vocabulary of page 20, they disagree about the construct: "legible to an investigator" versus "sufficient to reproduce the neuron's role."

How the standard is practiced when it is practiced

Two verified papers show what meeting the stronger standard looks like, and each also reports where it falls short.

Gao et al.'s "Scaling and evaluating sparse autoencoders" evaluates features by four metrics: downstream loss when the model runs on the reconstruction, probe loss against 61 labeled tasks, explainability ("are there simple explanations that are both necessary and sufficient for the activation"), and sparsity of ablation effects.[Gao SAE] Because "precision is extremely expensive to evaluate when the simulations are using GPT-4," they switch to a cheap n-gram explanation format that can be simulated on every token, hold out 16 activating sequences per feature for recall, and then use the explanations to simulate the features, decode, and measure downstream loss, a metric that "values both recall and precision in a way that is principled."[Gao SAE] That is Gao's reply as a procedure. The limitations are equally plain: the n-gram format "is only able to capture very simple patterns," and "a large fraction of the random activations of features we find, especially in GPT-4, are not yet adequately monosemantic."[Gao SAE]

Anthropic's "Towards Monosemanticity" triangulates. A blinded annotator scores features and neurons on a rubric across eleven activation intervals; Claude writes explanations and predicts activations on unseen tokens with "no leak of information about activation patterns, except for the explanation"; and a second held-out test asks whether the explanation predicts which output tokens the feature promotes, where features reach 74% against 58% for neurons and 50% for chance.[Monosemanticity] It also runs the page-28 control, features of a randomly initialized transformer, and reports the awkward outcome that the automated activation score ranked those random features higher, because single-token features are trivially simulable, while the rubric and the logit test ranked them correctly.[Monosemanticity] That is the standard in action: the control exposed a weakness of one instrument, and the write-up kept both in.

What the dispute teaches an evaluator

First, "demonstrated" is a property of a procedure, not a person. Cammarata co-authored the simulation-scoring paper and now argues against leading with it; Gao co-authored it and argues for a stricter version. Both positions come with methods attached, which is what lets a reader check them. Second, the fight is over the construct, as validity fights usually are: an audit that finds "one main job" for 90% of poles and a standard that demands behavior-preserving simulation measure different things, and a number from one does not refute a number from the other. Third, the cheap controls go missing more often than the expensive metrics. The essay reports a held-out test but, in the text fetched, no control-neuron comparison for it; the 2023 paper reported a baseline that beat its own method and left it in a results subsection. Reading for the control is the fastest way to find the soft spot in any interpretability claim, including your own.

Pitfall

Updating on the adjective. "Clean," "interpretable," "explained," "monosemantic" are conclusions, and the dispute above shows experts attaching them to different measurements. When a write-up says a neuron is explained, find the sentence that says what the explanation predicted, on which held-out data, with what score, against what control. If the sentence is "an agent investigated it and the story held together," you have a hypothesis; the essay's own words for its audit are "qualitative evidence."

Checklist: before a write-up says "explained"

  1. The estimand. Which unit (neuron, pole, feature), over which text distribution, scored how. If it is "fraction of units explained," the threshold and how the number moves with it.
  2. The explanation and its predictions. The exact sentence, and the activations it implies on sentences it has not seen, written before those sentences were run.
  3. The held-out set and its sampling. Texts the explainer never saw: random corpus text, across-the-range intervals, generated positives and near misses, or a search for high real activations the explanation missed. Random text alone barely sees false positives.
  4. The score and the simulator. The number, the simulator model, the scale, and whether it is activation correlation or an effect on the model's output.
  5. The control neuron. The same explanation against random units from the same layer, and the gap.
  6. The baseline explanation. What a single-token or surface-statistics explanation scores on the same set, and the margin over it.
  7. The shuffled-label null. How often a broken pairing scores as well, and on how many tokens.
  8. Both error types. Precision and recall, or their equivalents (fire-rate on generated positives and on near misses), reported separately.
  9. If the claim is causal, the intervention and its control. What changed when the unit was ablated or swapped, and what changed when a random unit was.
  10. The range covered. Evidence from the middle of the activation spectrum, not only the top examples, and the explanation's known failures.
  11. What would have made it lose. The falsifying result, stated in advance, and whether it was observed.
Key idea

The standard this course applied to graders, benchmarks and leaderboards applies unchanged to claims about what a model does inside: a claim is demonstrated when it made a prediction on data it had not seen, the prediction was scored, and the score beat the controls an empty claim would also pass. Everything else, from a persuasive gallery to a coauthor's intuition, is a hypothesis awaiting that test. Write the test before you look.
