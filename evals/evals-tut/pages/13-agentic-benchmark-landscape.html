The agentic benchmark landscape

Five more benchmarks, each read the way SWE-bench was: as an artifact whose design choices — environment, grading, human baseline, failure handling — determine what its numbers can mean. Each one is presented as the best exemplar of one design idea worth stealing.

GAIA: invert the difficulty, constrain the answer

GAIA (Mialon et al., 2023) poses 466 questions requiring "reasoning, multi-modality handling, web browsing, and generally tool-use proficiency" — find a fact buried in a video, cross-reference a paper with a database, compute something from a spreadsheet.[GAIA] Its first design idea is an inversion: instead of chasing tasks ever harder for humans, GAIA targets questions "conceptually simple for humans yet challenging for most advanced AIs." The measured gap proved the point: humans 92%, GPT-4 with plugins 15% — and by difficulty level (defined jointly by steps and tools required), GPT-4+plugins fell from 30.3% (Level 1) to 9.7% (Level 2) to 0% (Level 3) while humans stayed nearly flat (93.9 / 91.8 / 87.3%).[GAIA]

Its second idea is buying cheap, objective grading by constraining the answer format: every answer is a short factoid — "a string (one or a few words), a number, or a comma separated list" — scored by quasi-exact match with normalization.[GAIA] The trade is explicit and the authors also concede the cost: "GAIA does not evaluate the trace leading to the answer" — process is invisible to the grader.[GAIA] Note the defense built in: all 466 questions are public, but the answers to the 300 test questions are retained to power the leaderboard — a partial private holdout (Module 6 develops this design space).

WebArena: outcome rewards, and a probe for hallucinated success

WebArena (Zhou et al., ICLR 2024) gives agents a self-hosted miniature internet — fully functional replica sites for e-commerce, a Reddit-style forum, GitLab, and a CMS, plus a map and tools — and 812 tasks instantiated from 241 hand-curated templates.[WebArena] Self-hosting trades realism's edge cases for reproducibility and resettability — the right call for a shared instrument. Grading is programmatic functional correctness over the end state, which the authors argue "is not only more reliable than comparing the textual surface-form action sequences but also accommodate[s] a range of potential valid paths" — the outcome-vs-process choice from page 11, decided for outcomes.[WebArena] At publication: best GPT-4 agent 14.41%, humans 78.24%.[WebArena]

Two details deserve stealing. First, WebArena includes deliberately unachievable tasks: humans recognized 100% of them as impossible; GPT-4, only 44.44% — a designed probe for agents that hallucinate success rather than admit defeat.[WebArena] Second, per-template consistency exposes brittleness the headline hides: GPT-4 achieved 100% success on only 4 of 241 templates — everywhere else, success on one phrasing coexisted with failure on a paraphrase.[WebArena]

And the cautionary sequel

WebArena also illustrates what happens without a holdout. The Princeton audit found the top leaderboard agent, STeP, reached 35.8% partly by hardcoding policies for specific WebArena tasks (e.g., constructing Reddit URLs by pattern). The authors are careful to note STeP's developers had legitimate, different goals — but a leaderboard without a held-out set can't distinguish general capability from task-specific tailoring, and "the majority" of 17 agent benchmarks analyzed "do not include an appropriate held-out set."[Agents That Matter] Page 15 returns to this.

OSWorld: when the environment is a whole computer

OSWorld (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2024) scales the environment to a full operating system: 369 real computing tasks executed in an Ubuntu VM (plus an additional 43-task Windows set), spanning Chrome, VS Code, LibreOffice, GIMP, email, and the terminal.[OSWorld] Its design lesson is the price of valid grading in rich environments: every task needs a custom initial-state setup (302 distinct initial states) and a near-task-specific execution-based evaluation script — getter functions extract the relevant end state, evaluator functions judge it; 134 unique evaluation functions in all, at roughly two man-hours of development per evaluator.[OSWorld] Generic graders don't survive contact with real software; valid ones are hand-made.

The results carry two validity lessons. Humans scored 72.36% — measured, not assumed, and notably below perfect — while the best model managed 12.24%, and that with text-based accessibility-tree input; screenshot-only vision models topped out near 5–6%.[OSWorld] (Observation format moves the leaderboard — the harness lesson again.) And the aggregate hides a cliff: on multi-application workflow tasks agents generally scored below 5% while human performance stayed flat around 70% — an aggregate score averaging over a capability gap rather than revealing it.[OSWorld] OSWorld also adopted the infeasible-task probe: 30 impossible tasks where reward comes only from correctly declaring impossibility.[OSWorld]

AgentBench: breadth, and a failure taxonomy

AgentBench (Liu et al., ICLR 2024) went wide instead of deep: eight environments in three grounding categories — Code (operating system, database, knowledge graph), Game (card game, lateral-thinking puzzles, household), Web (shopping, browsing) — five of the eight purpose-built, with tasks taking 5–50 interaction rounds.[AgentBench] Its 2023-era headline — commercial API models averaged 2.32 vs 0.51 for open-source models up to 70B, a ~4.5× gap[AgentBench] — is a snapshot, not a law. What lasts is the failure taxonomy: the dominant failure mode was Task Limit Exceeded — agents floundering until the turn budget ran out — ahead of format and invalid-action errors, with "poor long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following" as the named obstacles.[AgentBench] Agents mostly don't crash; they wander. Which means turn limits are part of your metric: where you cap the episode decides how much wandering counts as failure.

Terminal-Bench: the living benchmark

Terminal-Bench (a Stanford × Laude Institute collaboration) tests agents on terminal tasks — compiling code, training models, configuring servers — and is the most instructive example of benchmark operations. Every task has four components: an English instruction, a Docker environment, a verification test script, and a reference "oracle" solution that must pass the tests — a built-in proof that the task is solvable and the verifier non-vacuous.[Terminal-Bench] Instead of freezing one dataset, it maintains a versioned registry — 1.0 (80 tasks), 2.0 (89 tasks), with 2.1 active and successors in development (counts as of June 2026)[Terminal-Bench] — treating the benchmark as software with releases, a lifecycle answer to contamination and saturation. And its leaderboard reports success rates with error bars — under which the top several agents overlap, ranking them statistically indistinguishable[Terminal-Bench] — a practice Module 5 will argue should be universal.

Key idea

Across the landscape, the same checklist decides what a score means: What does the grader literally check, and what is it blind to? Was the human baseline measured? Can the agent distinguish impossible from hard? Is there a holdout? Who verified the verifier? No benchmark answers all five perfectly — the skill is knowing which answers are missing when you read a leaderboard.

Pitfall

Citing any of these numbers without a date. Every figure on this page is a point-in-time measurement of specific 2023–2024 systems — WebArena's 14.41% and OSWorld's 12.24% were both left far behind by later agents, and Terminal-Bench's task counts change by design. The design lessons are durable; the leaderboard positions are perishable. Date-stamp every benchmark number you ever quote.
