Module 6 · Contamination & Validity

The political economy of leaderboards

The previous pages treated measurement failures as technical problems. This page treats them as incentive problems: what happens to an eval when billions of dollars ride on its rankings. The case study is the field's most influential leaderboard, Chatbot Arena, as audited in "The Leaderboard Illusion."

The instrument with the best validity story

Chatbot Arena seemed contamination-proof by construction: real users converse with two anonymous models, vote for the better answer, and Elo-style ratings aggregate millions of fresh, live preference battles — the design introduced alongside MT-Bench in 2023.[MT-Bench] No fixed test set to leak, questions invented continuously by users. If any public eval should resist the dynamics of Module 6, it's this one. "The Leaderboard Illusion" (Singh et al., 2025 — Cohere Labs with academic co-authors) audited the operational reality and found four distortions, none of which involve contaminated data.[Leaderboard Illusion]

Distortion 1: private testing and best-of-N disclosure

An unstated Arena policy allowed select providers to test multiple model variants privately — anonymously, with the option to retract scores — and publish only the winner. The documented extreme: 27 private variants tested by Meta on the main leaderboard in the lead-up to Llama 4 (March 2025 alone; the paper calls 27 conservative, counting 16 more on the vision leaderboard). No academic lab tested any private models in the observed period.[Leaderboard Illusion]

Why this breaks the math rather than just the optics: pick the maximum of N noisy scores and you've measured your luck, not your model. The authors demonstrated it with the cleanest possible experiment — submitting two identical checkpoints of their own model. The identical twins earned different Arena scores, 1069 vs 1052, with four other models ranked between them.[Leaderboard Illusion] Their simulation: privately testing just 10 variants buys roughly +100 points of expected maximum score — enough that "a weaker family of models … enabled with private testing can outperform a stronger family … restricted to a single submission."[Leaderboard Illusion] Rank, under this policy, partly measures testing strategy.

Distortions 2–4: data, fit, and quiet removal

  • Sampling asymmetry. Battles aren't distributed evenly: Google and OpenAI models received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all Arena data respectively; four proprietary providers together got 62.8% — 68× the combined share of Allen AI, Stanford, Princeton, and Berkeley — while 83 open-weight models split 29.7%.[Leaderboard Illusion] Battles are also data collection: every battle delivers user prompts and preference votes to the provider, compounding advantage.
  • Distribution fit masquerading as capability. In a controlled experiment, fine-tuning with progressively more Arena data (0% → 70% of a fixed budget) raised win-rate on ArenaHard — a test set drawn from the Arena distribution — from 23.5% to 49.9%, a relative gain of 112%. The same training showed limited benefit on MMLU: the gains were Arena-shaped, not general.[Leaderboard Illusion] That is Goodhart's law with a measured effect size — optimizing the proxy, detaching it from the construct (page 20).
  • Silent deprecation, asymmetrically applied. Of 243 public models, 205 had their sampling quietly cut to near zero — versus 47 officially listed as deprecated — and 64% of the silently deprecated were open-weight or open-source (deprecation rates ~88–89% for open models vs 80% proprietary).[Leaderboard Illusion] Beyond fairness, this corrodes the rating model itself: Elo-style (Bradley–Terry) rankings assume a connected graph of comparisons under a stable distribution, and selective removal breaks both.

The reforms — readable as design principles

The paper's five recommendations generalize to any eval that takes submissions: prohibit score retraction; publish hard limits on concurrent private variants (they suggest a maximum of three); apply deprecation policy uniformly across license types; sample fairly (prioritizing under-evaluated, high-variance pairs); and publish the full deprecation list.[Leaderboard Illusion] The common thread: the rules of participation are part of the instrument, and undisclosed rules are undisclosed measurement error.

Caveats — and an auditor's disclosure done right

This is an arXiv preprint whose lead authors work at Cohere — an Arena participant — a dual role they disclose explicitly (their own private-variant experiments appear in their data); the Arena's operators publicly disputed parts of the characterization after release. The data-share figures are estimates from scraped battle samples, not platform disclosures. None of that neutralizes the identical-checkpoint experiment or the simulations, which stand on their own math — but it does mean this page's framing comes from one side of a live dispute, and you should read the other side before quoting it in anger.[Leaderboard Illusion]

The view from inside a lab

It would be tidy to cast operators and providers as villains, but the better closing note for this module is Anthropic's own accounting of why evaluation is hard even with aligned incentives and internal access: multiple-choice benchmarks with label errors and ~5-point formatting sensitivity; third-party frameworks costing months of engineering (BIG-bench was "not 'plug and play'" — they found bugs and "dropped it after this experiment"); human evaluation that is expensive, slow, and gameable ("a system could avoid harm simply by providing unhelpful responses"); red-teaming that is "more art than science."[Anthropic] Their conclusion is institutional, matching this page's theme: fund the science of repeatable evaluations, fund implementations and robustness analysis, fund government evaluators like NIST, and create legal safe harbor for third-party evaluation.[Anthropic] Good measurement at this scale is infrastructure, and infrastructure needs governance.

Key idea — and the close of Module 6

A leaderboard is not a reading of nature; it's an institution — operating policies, sampling decisions, disclosure rules, participant incentives — that produces readings. Module 6's complete checklist for trusting any public number: Is the data uncontaminated (21)? Is the chain from construct to metric intact (20)? Are the defenses real (22)? And — this page — who runs the measurement, under what rules, and who benefits? Skepticism about that last question isn't cynicism; it's how the auditors in this module found everything they found.
