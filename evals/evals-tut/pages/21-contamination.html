Module 6 · Contamination & Validity

Data contamination

Models train on the internet; benchmarks live on the internet. When test data leaks into training data, scores measure familiarity instead of ability — and nothing about the score looks wrong. This page covers how contamination happens and the canonical demonstration of how to catch it: GSM1k.

How the test gets into the training set

Anthropic's analogy sets the stakes plainly: because MMLU is so widely used, "models are more likely to encounter MMLU questions during training. This is comparable to students seeing the questions before the test—it's cheating."[Anthropic] But "cheating" implies intent, and the mechanisms are mostly ambient:

  • Web-scrape inclusion. Benchmarks are published, mirrored, quoted in blog posts, embedded in tutorials — then scraped. This is why HumanEval's authors hand-wrote their 164 problems in 2021: GitHub "already contains solutions to problems from a variety of sources."[HumanEval]
  • The benchmark predates the model. Any benchmark built from pre-existing public artifacts is contaminated at birth for future models: 94% of SWE-bench's instances predate the training cutoffs of the models evaluated on them.[SWE-bench+]
  • The synthetic-data side door. Training on LLM-generated data resembling a benchmark inflates scores without the test set ever being touched — a verified case below.
  • Solution leakage. The answer sits in the task input itself — one-third of audited SWE-bench successes (page 12). Not training contamination, but the same effect: score without skill.

GSM1k: the controlled experiment

By 2024, GSM8k — the standard grade-school math benchmark — was years old, public, and suspiciously conquered. Scale AI's GSM1k study (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024) ran the experiment everyone needed: commission fresh problems that are like the benchmark in every way except being new, and see which models' scores survive.

The construction is the masterclass. Human annotators, prompted with example GSM8k problems, wrote novel ones; a pipeline of independent re-solving, quality audits, and winnowing to match GSM8k's answer-magnitude distribution took 2,108 raw problems down to a final 1,205 — at a cost of roughly $180,000 in annotation fees.[GSM1k] Then they validated the difficulty match three independent ways: human annotators shown one GSM1k problem among four GSM8k problems picked the newcomer only 21.83% of the time (chance: 20%); timed humans solved slightly more GSM1k problems per 15 minutes than GSM8k (4.36 vs 4.07 — the new set is, if anything, easier, making any score drops conservative); and pre-2021 models that provably never saw either dataset (GPT-2, GPT-NeoX-20B) scored the same on both.[GSM1k] That third check is the quiet brilliance: a control group for the benchmark itself.

What they found

  • Systematic family-level overfitting. "Accuracy drops of up to 8%," with the Phi and Mistral families performing worse on GSM1k "for almost every release and scale," and Yi, Xwin, Gemma, and CodeLlama showing the pattern to a lesser degree.[GSM1k] Worst individual cases: Yi-6B-Chat −8.0, math-shepherd-mistral-7b-rl −7.2, phi-2 −6.3 points.[GSM1k]
  • Frontier models came out clean. GPT, Gemini, Claude, and Mistral Large performed similarly on both sets — Claude actually slightly better on the fresh problems. The authors' favored explanation: "sufficiently strong LLMs also learn elementary reasoning ability during training" — supported by the telling detail that Mistral Large is the only clean Mistral model, and selective decontamination of just the largest model is implausible.[GSM1k]
  • An independent contamination signal agreed. A model's per-character log-likelihood of generating GSM8k text — how unsurprised it is by the exact test data — correlated with its GSM8k-vs-GSM1k gap (Spearman rank correlation 0.36, p = 0.03).[GSM1k] Models that found the benchmark text familiar were the models whose scores didn't survive fresh instances.
  • But contamination wasn't the whole story. One of the most overfit models (Math-Shepherd) had low GSM8k log-likelihood — it was trained on synthetic data resembling the benchmark, not the benchmark itself. The authors' caution: "data contamination is likely not the full story."[GSM1k]
  • Overfit ≠ unable. The most-quoted nuance: phi-2 dropped ~6 points yet still solved over half of GSM1k — problems guaranteed absent from its training data. "The fact that a model is overfit does not mean that it is poor at reasoning, merely that it is not as good as the benchmarks might indicate it to be."[GSM1k] Contamination inflates; it doesn't fabricate from nothing.
A meta-lesson this course caught in the act

The GSM1k numbers most people quote are wrong — because they cite the May 2024 preprint: 1,250 problems, drops "up to 13%," correlation 0.32. The final NeurIPS version revised all three: 1,205 problems, up to 8%, 0.36.[GSM1k] This page's claims were verified against the camera-ready. The lesson generalizes: preprints mutate, and a citation without a version is a citation to a moving target — the reproducibility rules of page 19 apply to papers, not just code.

The reusable recipe

GSM1k's method transfers to any benchmark you depend on, and its components are worth listing as a checklist because each one answers a specific objection:

  1. Fresh instances, matched in distribution — style, difficulty, answer format. (Objection answered: "your new problems are just harder.")
  2. Empirical difficulty validation — human indistinguishability, timed solve rates. (Same objection, with receipts.)
  3. An uncontaminated control — models that predate both datasets should show no gap. (Objection: "your pipeline differs somehow.")
  4. An independent signal — the log-likelihood probe. (Objection: "the gap is coincidence.")
  5. Keep the new set private, with a committed release trigger — GSM1k's authors withheld the dataset, running recurring evaluations, with release promised when top open models exceed 95% or by a fixed date.[GSM1k] (Objection: "now your benchmark will contaminate.")

The same logic, adapted to agents, produced the SWE-Bench Illusion's in-benchmark vs fresh-same-repo vs outside-repo differentials (page 12) — and the next page covers benchmarks that build the freshness in from the start.

Key idea

Contamination can't be ruled out by inspection — the score looks identical either way. It can only be ruled out by design (data the model cannot have seen) or exposed by experiment (fresh instances, matched and validated). Any high score on an old public benchmark carries an asterisk that only one of those two moves can remove.
