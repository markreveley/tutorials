Module 3 · LLM-as-Judge

Aligning judges with human experts

A judge earns trust one way: by demonstrably agreeing with your domain expert on outputs neither has seen before. This page covers the workflow that gets you there — critique shadowing — and the measurement that proves you arrived, with the statistics chosen so they don't flatter you.

Critique shadowing: the workflow

Husain's name for the process is critique shadowing: the judge learns to shadow a specific expert's judgment, expressed as binary verdicts plus written critiques. The seven verified steps:[Husain, Judge]

  1. Find the principal domain expert — the "one (maybe two) key individuals whose judgment is crucial." Husain is specific about why developers shouldn't stand in: "People will have varying opinions about what is acceptable, and you can't make everyone happy. What's important is that your principal domain expert is satisfied."
  2. Create a dataset — diverse, structured by dimensions (features × scenarios × personas), real plus synthetic (page 5).
  3. Expert makes pass/fail judgments with critiques — each critique "detailed enough that a new employee could understand it"; terseness is the common failure.
  4. Fix obvious errors — some failures need a bug fix, not a judge; loop back to step 3 with fresh outputs.
  5. Build the judge iteratively — expert critiques become few-shot examples; test the judge against expert verdicts; refine the prompt "until agreement is satisfactory."
  6. Perform error analysis — apply the judge to unseen data, compute failure rates by dimension, classify root causes.
  7. Create specialized judges — if needed, split criteria into separate scoped judges.

The loop has no exit: "This process never truly ends. It repeats periodically or when material changes occur."[Husain, Judge] That's criteria drift showing up as an operations schedule.

A verified case study, with its caveat attached

The concrete numbers come from Husain's work on Honeycomb's natural-language query assistant, with domain expert Phillip Carter. The mechanics were deliberately low-tech: a spreadsheet, sent every few days, with 25–50 examples — each row showing the user's query, the generated result, the judge's critique and verdict; Phillip filled in his own verdicts and critiques.[Husain, Evals] Disagreements drove prompt revisions. Tracked over iterations: "It took us only three iterations to achieve >90% agreement between the LLM and Phillip."[Husain, Judge]

The caveat is half the lesson

Husain immediately qualifies the metric: raw agreement was acceptable only because the dataset was roughly balanced ("about 50% of instances were failures"); in general, "using raw agreement … can be misleading when classes are imbalanced. Instead, you should typically measure precision and recall separately."[Husain, Judge] Why: if only 5% of outputs fail, a judge that passes everything scores 95% agreement while catching zero failures. (Worked example in Appendix E.) And the "three iterations" figure is one case study — "Your mileage may vary depending on the complexity of the task."

Measuring alignment without flattering yourself

The measurement stack, from minimum to most rigorous — all computed on a held-out human-labeled set the judge never saw during prompt iteration:

MetricWhat it answersWhen it misleads
Raw agreementHow often do judge and expert match?Class imbalance; chance agreement
TPR + TNRCatch rate on real failures; pass rate on real passes — separatelyRarely — this is the recommended pair[Evals FAQ]
Cohen's kappaAgreement beyond chanceSmall samples; but strictly more honest than raw agreement

The FAQ's verified protocol uses the middle row: "Focus on achieving high True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) with your judge on a held out labeled test set" — and then puts those rates to work: "Once you know your judge's True Positive and True Negative rates, you can correct its estimates to determine the actual failure rate in your system."[Evals FAQ] That correction step is underused and valuable: an imperfect-but-characterized judge yields an unbiased estimate of the true failure rate, because you know exactly how it errs.

Kappa is the honesty upgrade on raw agreement: it subtracts the agreement two graders would reach by chance. Yan calls it "a more precise and conservative measurement of alignment compared to percentage agreement" and reports the sobering reference points: GPT-4 and Llama-3-70B achieved kappa of 0.84 and 0.79 against humans — good, yet "still significantly lower than the human-human Cohen's κ of 0.97" in that study.[Yan] Recall the MT-Bench percentage story from page 8 — 85% vs 81%, judge beats human — then notice that under a chance-corrected lens the same kind of comparison can invert. Which metric you choose is itself a measurement decision. For graded (non-binary) outputs, the corresponding tools are rank correlations — Spearman's rho or Kendall's tau.[Yan]

Maintenance: alignment decays

The verified cadence: re-run the human-comparison cycle "at regular intervals and whenever something material changes" — a model swap, a prompt overhaul, a new feature. After the first couple of iterations, shift sampling toward suspected errors plus a random slice, so expert attention concentrates where the judge is most likely wrong.[Husain, Judge] Budget for this up front: the standing cost of a judge is "100+ labeled examples, ongoing weekly maintenance, and coordination between developers, PMs, and domain experts."[Evals FAQ] The expert never disappears from the loop, but their workload shrinks to "a representative sample so you can have reliable statistics" — because, as Husain puts it, "the LLM still needs to be aligned to something, and that something is usually a human."[Husain, Judge]

Do you really need all this?

Sometimes no. Husain's own shortcut criteria: if you are the domain expert (solo developer), or your data is cheap to review by eye, run the lightweight version — grade outputs yourself, keep notes, skip the formal judge until scale forces it.[Husain, Judge] The machinery exists to scale expert judgment, not to ritualize it.

Pitfall

Iterating the judge prompt against the same labeled examples you'll report agreement on. After enough revisions, the prompt has effectively memorized the answer key — overfitting, in classical form — and the reported agreement is fiction. Hold out a slice of expert labels from day one; report agreement only on that slice; refresh it when you re-validate. (The same mistake at benchmark scale is Module 6's entire subject.)

Key idea

A judge is a measuring instrument with a calibration certificate: who it was aligned to, on what data, measured how (TPR/TNR, kappa), and when it expires. No certificate, no trust. With one, an imperfect judge becomes a known instrument whose readings you can correct — which is all any real instrument ever is.
