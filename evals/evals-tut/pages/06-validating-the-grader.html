Validating the grader

You built automated graders so you could stop hand-reviewing everything. But a grader is itself a system that can be wrong — so who grades it? This page covers the research that made that question precise: the EvalGen study, its "criteria drift" finding, and what both imply about how eval criteria can ever be trusted.

The validator's regress

The promise of automated evaluation has a circularity at its center. The EvalGen paper — Shankar and colleagues, published at UIST 2024 — states it in one line: "LLM-generated evaluators simply inherit all the problems of the LLMs they evaluate, requiring further human validation."[EvalGen] An unvalidated judge isn't a measurement instrument; it's a second opinion from a colleague of the defendant. The regress has to stop somewhere, and it stops at human judgment — the question is how to spend scarce human judgment so it transfers to the automated layer.

EvalGen: aligning assertions with human grades

EvalGen is the paper's prototype interface for doing exactly that. The workflow: an LLM suggests evaluation criteria for your pipeline; for each criterion it generates several candidate implementations — Python assertions or LLM judge prompts; meanwhile the human grades a small sample of real outputs (binary good/bad). The system then selects, per criterion, the candidate implementation whose verdicts best match the human's grades, and shows a report card of how aligned the chosen graders are.[EvalGen]

"Best match" is defined with care worth learning from. For an assertion set F, the paper defines alignment from two components, both measured against the human's grades:

Alignment(F) = 2 · Coverage · (1 − FFR) / (Coverage + (1 − FFR))

where coverage is the fraction of human-graded-bad outputs that at least one assertion catches, and the false failure rate (FFR) is the fraction of human-graded-good outputs that some assertion wrongly fails. The formula is the harmonic mean of the two — "very similar to F1 score; however, we are concerned with the precision and recall of failures."[EvalGen] (Harmonic means and precision/recall are taught from zero in Appendix E.) Two different mistakes — missing real failures, and crying wolf on good outputs — are kept separate and both punished. Any grader validation scheme you build should preserve that two-sidedness.

Does human-in-the-loop selection actually beat full automation? The paper's quantitative comparison says yes: against SPADE, a fully automated assertion-generation baseline, EvalGen "achieved equal or better alignment … with fewer assertions," raising coverage from 49% to 73% on one pipeline with an assertion set less than half the size — using only 16 human-graded outputs per pipeline.[EvalGen] Sixteen careful grades is a lunch break. The leverage of small amounts of expert judgment, spent at the right point in the pipeline, is the recurring economic fact of this whole module.

Criteria drift: the finding that reframes everything

The paper's most influential result, though, came from watching nine industry practitioners use EvalGen in a think-aloud study. The expectation baked into most eval tooling is a waterfall: decide your criteria, then grade outputs against them. What the study observed instead is now called criteria drift: "Grading outputs spurred changes or refinements in participants' criteria," in two distinct ways.[EvalGen]

  • New criteria appeared when participants saw new kinds of bad output they hadn't anticipated.
  • Existing criteria got reinterpreted as grading continued — one participant's "all entities are proper nouns" relaxed to "most entities are proper nouns" once real outputs made the strict version untenable. Some participants went back and changed their own earlier grades.[EvalGen]

Underneath both is what the authors dub a catch-22: "to grade outputs, people need to externalize and define their evaluation criteria; however, the process of grading outputs helps them to define that very criteria." Their conclusion is stated strongly and is worth quoting exactly, because it is the theoretical justification for this module's entire data-first ordering: criteria drift "implies that it is impossible to completely determine evaluation criteria prior to human judging of LLM outputs."[EvalGen] Or as participant P7 put it: "I think it's hard to know until you see it."

Why this explains earlier advice

Criteria drift is the load-bearing fact behind three positions you've already met: error analysis before metrics (page 4), "generally no" to eval-driven development (page 5), and open-coding 30–50 traces yourself before delegating to an LLM. All three are the same derived rule: criteria are an output of contact with real data, so any process that fixes them in advance is building on guesses.

Alignment is alignment with someone

A quieter finding deserves more attention than it gets. Two participants ended up with the identical code assertion for the same criterion — a check for hashtag characters — and it aligned with one participant's grades but not the other's. The cause: "tacit criteria that participants held which was not explicitly explained in natural language."[EvalGen] The same words meant different things to different experts. There is no grader that is "aligned" in the abstract — only aligned with a particular person's judgment. This is the empirical case for the principal domain expert pattern from page 3: one designated judgment to align to, rather than an average of several that quietly disagree.

Participants also wanted different strictness for different criteria — "there are criteria where you can be okay with failing, and then there are other criteria where you are like, 'this must absolutely pass'"[EvalGen] — a reminder that a single global threshold over all your graders flattens real differences in stakes. A formatting check and a safety check should not share a tolerance.

Caveat

Know the shape of this evidence: the criteria-drift study is qualitative — nine practitioners, one session each, one task (entity extraction over 100 tweets), with each drift type observed in five of the nine participants.[EvalGen] That's the right method for discovering a phenomenon, not for estimating its prevalence. The finding has spread widely because it names something practitioners recognize instantly — but treat "criteria always drift" as a strong working hypothesis confirmed by experience, not a measured universal.

What validating-the-grader looks like in practice

  1. Grade a sample yourself first (binary + critique) — your grades are the standard the grader must hit, and grading is also how your criteria finish forming.
  2. Measure the grader against your grades both ways — its catch rate on real failures and its false-alarm rate on good outputs, on a held-out set it never saw during development (page 10 gives the full workflow).
  3. Expect drift, schedule re-validation — when your criteria shift, yesterday's aligned grader is silently grading the old rules. Re-grade a fresh sample at every error-analysis cycle and re-check agreement.
  4. Set per-criterion thresholds — strict for must-never-fail properties, tolerant where failures are cheap.
Key idea

"Who validates the validators?" has a real answer: a specific human's judgment, captured in a modest number of binary grades with critiques, measured against the grader two-sidedly, and refreshed as the criteria themselves evolve. Validation isn't a one-time certification — it's a standing subscription, because the thing being validated against (your own understanding of quality) is a moving target.
