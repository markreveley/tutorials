Annotated bibliography

The course's complete citation universe. Every factual claim in every page traces to one of these sources, and every source was verified by fetching the actual paper, post, or documentation — not recalled from memory. Annotations note what each source uniquely teaches and, where our verification caught the record diverging from the folklore, what to watch for.

How these were verified

Each source was fetched in full (arXiv PDFs/HTML, live documentation, archived repositories) during June 2026, and the specific claims the course planned to make were checked against the text — confirmed, corrected, or discarded. The structured verification notes, with exact quotes and locations, live in the repository under research/sources/. Corrections worth knowing are flagged ⚠ below.

The canonical process core

Shreya Shankar & Hamel Husain, Evals for AI Engineers: Systematically Measuring and Improving AI Applications, O'Reilly Media. oreilly.com
The spine text for Modules 1–2: the Three Gulfs framework and the Analyze–Measure–Improve lifecycle, with dedicated chapters on error analysis, automated evaluators, RAG, agents, and CI/CD. ⚠ As of June 2026 it is an Early Release (final publication October 2026) — chapter content may still change; there is no chapter called "judges" (LLM-as-judge lives inside Ch. 5, "Implementing Automated Evaluators").

Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar, "LLM Evals: Everything You Need to Know" (the Evals FAQ), hamel.dev, January 2026. hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq
The fastest map of the field's practical disputes, with deliberately sharp positions: binary over Likert, 100+ traces to saturation (~20-trace stopping heuristic), "no" to generic metrics, the benevolent-dictator labeling model, 60–80% of dev time on evals. ⚠ Retitled from "Frequently Asked Questions (And Answers) About AI Evals"; takes no position on fine-tuning vs prompting judges, despite frequent citation for one.

Hamel Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals", hamel.dev, March 2024. hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals
The original error-analysis-first argument: the three levels (unit tests; human & model eval; A/B testing), the Rechat/Lucy case study, and "remove all friction from looking at data." ⚠ The assistant is Lucy (company: Rechat) — "Lucid" is a common misquote.

Hamel Husain, "Using LLM-as-a-Judge For Evaluation: A Complete Guide", hamel.dev, October 2024. hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge
The Critique Shadowing method in seven steps, the Principal Domain Expert, the Honeycomb case (>90% agreement in three iterations — with the essential caveat that raw agreement only worked because classes were balanced). ⚠ Originally titled "Creating a LLM-as-a-Judge That Drives Business Results"; the phrase "benevolent dictator" does not appear in it.

Shreya Shankar, J.D. Zamfirescu-Pereira, Björn Hartmann, Aditya G. Parameswaran, Ian Arawjo, "Who Validates the Validators? Aligning LLM-Assisted Evaluation of LLM Outputs with Human Preferences", UIST 2024 (DOI 10.1145/3654777.3676450). arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272
The EvalGen paper — the academic anchor for grader validation: criteria drift (two types, observed in a 9-participant study), the grading catch-22, and alignment as the harmonic mean of coverage and (1 − false-failure-rate). Qualitative evidence; treat prevalence claims accordingly.

"AI Evals for Engineers & PMs" (Shankar & Husain), Maven. maven.com/parlance-labs/evals
The live course the book grew from. ⚠ Current scale: 4,500+ engineers and PMs across cohorts — the often-quoted "700+" is stale.

LLM-as-judge: mechanics and failure modes

Lianmin Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", NeurIPS 2023. arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685
The paper that legitimized LLM judges and catalogued their biases. Teach the numbers with their setups: 85% vs 81% agreement is the non-tie S2 setup; position-bias consistency 23.8%–65.0% by judge; the verbosity attack (91.3/91.3/8.7% failure). ⚠ Self-enhancement bias is explicitly inconclusive in the paper — don't cite it as demonstrated.

Arjun Panickssery, Samuel R. Bowman, Shi Feng, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations", NeurIPS 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076
The empirical core of self-preference: out-of-box self-recognition (GPT-4: 73.5%), fine-tuning to >90% recognition increases self-preference linearly, with control fine-tunes ruling out confounds; the label-swap experiment is the vivid demo. ⚠ The authors state this is evidence toward the causal hypothesis, not proof; GPT-4 was never fine-tuned.

Eugene Yan — "Patterns for Building LLM-based Systems" (2023); "Task-Specific LLM Evals that Do & Don't Work" (2024); "Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators" (2024), eugeneyan.com. eugeneyan.com
The best practitioner synthesis outside the Parlance orbit: evals as pattern #1, classification metrics over bare accuracy, binary judge outputs, Cohen's kappa over raw agreement (judge kappas 0.84/0.79 vs human-human 0.97), and the sobering detection-gap numbers (30–60% recall on inconsistent summaries; 58.5% on HaluEval).

Agentic evaluation

Sayash Kapoor, Benedikt Stroebl, Zachary S. Siegel, Nitya Nadgir, Arvind Narayanan, "AI Agents That Matter", arXiv:2407.01502, Princeton, 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2407.01502
The critique of agent benchmarking: cost-controlled evaluation and Pareto frontiers (simple baselines match SOTA agents at up to 50× less cost — match, not beat), the four-level holdout framework (5 of 17 benchmarks adequate), and the five reproducibility root causes.

Carlos E. Jimenez, John Yang et al., "SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues?", ICLR 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770
The template for execution-graded agent benchmarks: 2,294 instances from real PRs, resolved = patch applies + all tests pass. ⚠ The famous 1.96% (Claude 2) is a BM25-retrieval number; oracle retrieval gives 4.8% — the headline always contained a harness.

OpenAI, "Introducing SWE-bench Verified", openai.com, August 2024. openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified
The benchmark repair: 93 annotators, 1,699 tasks screened, 68.3% filtered out (38.3% underspecified, 61.1% unfair tests — overlapping), 500 kept; GPT-4o's score doubled (16% → 33.2%) with no model change.

Reem Aleithan et al., "SWE-Bench+", arXiv:2410.06992, 2024; Shanchao Liang, Spandan Garg, Roshanak Zilouchian Moghaddam, "The SWE-Bench Illusion", arXiv:2506.12286 (v4), 2025. arxiv.org/abs/2410.06992, arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286
The two-wave critique: solution leakage in ~33% of resolved instances and weak tests in ~31% (the 12.47% → 3.97% → 0.55% arc); then the memorization diagnostics (76% file-path identification from issue text alone vs ≤53% out-of-benchmark; verbatim solution reproduction rising with model generation). Both are preprints; both audited GPT-4-era agents.

Grégoire Mialon et al., "GAIA: a benchmark for General AI Assistants", arXiv:2311.12983, 2023. arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983
The difficulty inversion (humans 92% vs GPT-4+plugins 15%) and the factoid-answer design that buys cheap valid scoring. ⚠ Questions are public; only the 300 test answers are withheld. The paper concedes it cannot grade the trace.

Shunyu Yao, Noah Shinn, Pedram Razavi, Karthik Narasimhan, "τ-bench", arXiv:2406.12045, Sierra, 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045
User simulation + state-based grading + the pass^k reliability metric: gpt-4o at 61.2% average on retail falls below 25% at pass^8. The policy-ablation experiment (retail −4.4 vs airline −22.4) is a model probe of what a score measures.

Shuyan Zhou et al., "WebArena", ICLR 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2307.13854
Self-hosted web environments with outcome-based reward functions; 812 tasks from 241 templates; 14.41% vs human 78.24% at publication; the unachievable-task probe (humans 100%, GPT-4 44.44%).

Tianbao Xie et al., "OSWorld", NeurIPS 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2404.07972
The price of valid grading in rich environments: 369 Ubuntu tasks, 302 initial states, 134 hand-built evaluation functions (~2 person-hours each); measured human baseline 72.36% vs best model 12.24% (accessibility-tree input, not screenshots).

Xiao Liu et al., "AgentBench", ICLR 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2308.03688
Breadth (8 environments, 3 groundings) and the failure taxonomy: the dominant agent failure is Task Limit Exceeded — wandering, not crashing. The commercial-vs-OSS gap (2.32 vs 0.51) is a dated 2023 snapshot.

Terminal-Bench (Stanford × Laude Institute), tbench.ai, accessed June 2026. tbench.ai
The living-benchmark exemplar: four-component tasks (instruction, Docker env, test script, oracle solution), a versioned dataset registry (1.0: 80 tasks; 2.0: 89), and a leaderboard with error bars under which top agents overlap.

Thomas Kwa, Ben West et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks", arXiv:2503.14499 (v3, Feb 2026). arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499
The time-horizon metric (human task duration at 50% model success), built on 2,529 hours of human baselining and IRT-style logistic fits; doubling every ~207 days; 80%-reliability horizons are 4–6× shorter. ⚠ Headline numbers differ by paper version (v1: Claude 3.7 ~50 min; v3: o3 ~110 min) — date-stamp citations.

Statistical rigor

Evan Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals", arXiv:2411.00640, Anthropic, 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640
The single best practical paper on eval statistics: super-population framing, CLT standard errors, clustered SEs (up to 3.05× larger on real data), paired comparisons, resampling (and "don't touch the thermostat"), and the power-analysis basis for the ~1,000-question rule. The Galleon/Dreadnought example — proper statistics reversing the naive verdict — is the module in miniature. ⚠ AI-generated summaries of this paper circulate with confabulated numbers; ours were verified against the PDF.

Mark Chen et al., "Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code" (HumanEval), arXiv:2107.03374, 2021. arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374
The unbiased pass@k estimator (the naive plug-in is a consistent underestimate), the BLEU-fails-for-code demonstration, hand-written problems as contamination defense, and temperature-per-k. pass@k itself originates with Kulal et al. 2019.

Stella Biderman, Hailey Schoelkopf, Lintang Sutawika et al. (EleutherAI), "Lessons from the Trenches on Reproducible Evaluation of Language Models", arXiv:2405.14782 (v3). arxiv.org/abs/2405.14782
The reproducibility failure catalogue from the lm-evaluation-harness maintainers: ~46-point prompt-format swings with rank reversals, three MMLU implementations disagreeing, answer-extraction and tokenization traps, and the best-practices checklist. ⚠ Quote against v3; the 2024 v1 lacks some sections.

Contamination and validity

Hugh Zhang et al. (Scale AI), "A Careful Examination of Large Language Model Performance on Grade School Arithmetic" (GSM1k), NeurIPS 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332
The canonical contamination demonstration and reusable recipe: 1,205 fresh difficulty-validated problems, family-level overfitting (Phi, Mistral; up to 8 points), clean frontier models, the log-likelihood probe (Spearman 0.36), and "overfit ≠ can't reason." ⚠ The widely-quoted 1,250 problems / 13% drops / r²=0.32 are preprint numbers, all revised in the camera-ready.

Colin White et al., "LiveBench: A Challenging, Contamination-Limited LLM Benchmark", ICLR 2025. arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314
The rotating-benchmark design (1/6 monthly refresh, rolling private sixth, rank stability >0.997) and the evidence against LLM judges on hard reasoning (error rates 10–46%, score inflation). ⚠ Retitled from "Contamination-Free" to "Contamination-Limited" — the correction is itself the lesson.

Naman Jain et al., "LiveCodeBench", ICLR 2025. arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974
Date-tagged problems and "scrolling through time" as contamination detection: the DeepSeek post-cutoff cliff (base model ~60% → ~0% on LeetCode), with the controls that make it causal (AtCoder stable; pre-window models flat). Also the HumanEval-overfitting scatter.

ARC Prize Foundation, ARC-AGI dataset documentation and model testing policy, arcprize.org, accessed June 2026. arcprize.org/arc-agi/2, arcprize.org/policy
The four-way split architecture (public train / public eval / semi-private / private), difficulty-calibrated to <1pp so cross-split agreement works as a live overfitting alarm; plus the "soft contamination" warning. ⚠ Semi-private is a managed trust compromise, not a sealed holdout; task counts differ between ARC-AGI-1 and -2.

Shivalika Singh et al., "The Leaderboard Illusion", arXiv:2504.20879 (v2), 2025. arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879
The Arena-meta critique: undisclosed private testing (Meta: 27 variants), the identical-checkpoint experiment (1052 vs 1069), sampling asymmetries (top four proprietary providers: 62.8% of data), Arena-data fine-tuning gains that don't transfer (112% relative on ArenaHard, flat on MMLU), and silent deprecation. ⚠ Preprint by Arena participants (disclosed); the platform disputed parts — read both sides.

BIG-bench collaboration, canary string documentation, github.com/google/BIG-bench (archived 2026). github.com/google/BIG-bench
The practical canary mechanism: one shared GUID for filtering plus a two-tailed log-probability hypothesis test for post-hoc detection (GPT-2 sanity check: p ≈ 0.18). ⚠ A clean canary score does not prove a clean model — untagged copies escape it.

Anthropic, "Challenges in Evaluating AI Systems", anthropic.com, October 2023. anthropic.com/news/evaluating-ai-systems
A lab's own honest accounting: ~5-point MMLU formatting sensitivity, mislabeled "ground truth," the BBQ bias-score-zero control-check story, BIG-bench/HELM integration pain, and policy recommendations. Advocacy-adjacent; pre-dates current eval infrastructure.

Tooling

UK AI Security Institute, Inspect documentation, inspect.aisi.org.uk, accessed June 2026. inspect.aisi.org.uk
The primary harness: Task = Dataset/Solver/Scorer, sandboxed agents, the full scorer spectrum, epochs with pass_at_k reducers, binary .eval logs with a live viewer, human baselining and approval. ⚠ "AI Security Institute" since February 2025 (formerly Safety); no built-in judge-vs-human agreement workflow — that discipline remains yours.

OpenAI Evals, github.com/openai/evals + Cookbook, accessed June 2026. github.com/openai/evals — The first-generation blueprint: YAML registry, JSONL data, deterministic vs model-graded templates. In maintenance mode; OpenAI recommends its hosted product.

promptfoo documentation, promptfoo.dev, accessed June 2026. promptfoo.dev/docs — The prompts × providers × tests matrix, the fullest public assertion taxonomy (deterministic and model-assisted, including agent-trajectory assertions), and first-class red-teaming.

DeepEval (Confident AI) and Ragas (Exploding Gradients) documentation, accessed June 2026. deepeval.com/docs, docs.ragas.io — Metric libraries; "almost all" DeepEval metrics are LLM-as-judge; the four Ragas RAG metrics with their actual algorithms. ⚠ Same metric names compute differently across tools; DeepEval's Hallucination and Faithfulness have opposite polarity; Ragas now says "Response Relevancy."

Langfuse and Braintrust documentation, accessed June 2026. langfuse.com/docs, braintrust.dev/docs — The tracing/observability layer: traces, observations (spans/generations/events), scores on traces/sessions/observations, immutable experiments over versioned datasets, and the unified offline/online loop.

Evaluating explanations (Module 8)

Steven Bills, Nick Cammarata, Dan Mossing, Henk Tillman, Leo Gao, Gabriel Goh, Ilya Sutskever, Jan Leike, Jeff Wu, William Saunders, "Language models can explain neurons in language models", OpenAI, May 2023. openaipublic.blob.core.windows.net/neuron-explainer/paper
The simulation-scoring method (explain, simulate, score by correlation) and its honest numbers for GPT-2 XL: average 0.151 top-and-random, 1.7% of 307,200 neurons above 0.7, 0.06% above 0.9, humans only modestly better. Read the limitations: scoring is recall-heavy, a per-token lookup table beat the method on average, and the score is correlational (ρ of 0.9 gave ablation scores of 0.3–0.6). ⚠ The page is a JavaScript bundle; the prose had to be extracted from index.bundle.js. The phrase "trivial reasons" is the course's gloss, not the paper's.

Jing Huang, Atticus Geiger, Karel D'Oosterlinck, Zhengxuan Wu, Christopher Potts, "Rigorously Assessing Natural Language Explanations of Neurons", arXiv:2309.10312, September 2023. arxiv.org/abs/2309.10312
The two test modes, observational ("all and only") and interventional (causal mediation), applied to the top-scoring Bills explanations: precision 0.64, recall 0.50, F1 0.56, random pairing 0; F1 correlates −0.1 with the simulation score; GPT-4-selected neurons about as causally effective as random ones. The 5n% argument for why five random excerpts cannot see a rare false positive.

Leo Gao, Tom Dupré la Tour, Henk Tillman, Gabriel Goh, Rajan Troll, Alec Radford, Ilya Sutskever, Jan Leike, Jeffrey Wu, "Scaling and evaluating sparse autoencoders", arXiv:2406.04093, June 2024. arxiv.org/abs/2406.04093
Four feature-quality metrics (downstream loss, probe loss, explainability as precision and recall, ablation sparsity), the "don't stop"/"stop" example of a recall-heavy score, the zero-ablation baseline that makes anything look 98% faithful, and explanation reconstruction as a metric that prices both error types. The 16-million-feature GPT-4 autoencoder. ⚠ Its own limitations: n-gram explanations capture only simple patterns; many GPT-4 features are "not yet adequately monosemantic."

Trenton Bricken, Adly Templeton, Joshua Batson, Brian Chen, Adam Jermyn et al. (Anthropic), "Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning", Transformer Circuits Thread, October 2023. transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features
Triangulated evaluation: a blinded rubric sampled across eleven activation intervals (median neuron 0, median feature interval 12), automated explanation with an explicit no-leak held-out prediction, a logit-weight prediction test (74% vs 58%, chance 50%), and a randomized-transformer control that exposed the automated activation score (random features scored higher). ⚠ The automated correlation distributions are in figures only; the course cites no median from them. Page is 20 MB; fetched with curl.

Nick Cammarata (Goodfire), "Neural Networks Are Already Clean", informal research essay, 2026. already-clean.goodfire.com
The case-study claim: preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B with ~90% of neuron poles having "one main job," a 200-neuron prediction test (new examples fire strongly 76% vs 11% for near misses; won 164, tied 15, lost 4), and an argument against leading with simulation scores. ⚠ Self-described working hypothesis; numbers are threshold-dependent by the author's own account; the trillion-parameter (Kimi K3) result is asserted and deferred to a companion paper, with "full audits of this scale" not begun; no random-neuron comparison for the prediction test appears in the fetched text. Not peer reviewed as far as can be determined.

Leo Gao (@nabla_theta), reply to Nick Cammarata on X, 15 October 2026. x.com/nabla_theta/status/2110862078844272677
The demonstrated-explanation standard in one line: "imo if you can't simulate a neuron you haven't really explained it," with the proposal to score by KL divergence of the model's output rather than activation correlation. ⚠ Retrieved through mirror APIs (x.com refused a direct fetch); the roadmap's phrasing of the critique is a paraphrase; the longer post it promises was not located and is not cited.
