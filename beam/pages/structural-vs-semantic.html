Rung 7 · applied — the payoff

Structural vs Semantic

Here's where the whole descent pays off. Everything below the Elixir surface — bytes, words, representation — usually stays invisible. But sometimes it leaks into observable behavior, and if you don't have the lower model, the behavior looks like a bug. The classic case: sorting strings.

The puzzle

In Elixir, "alien" < "office" is true — fine, alphabetical. But "álien" < "office" is false: the accented word sorts after "office", past o, p, … z. One accent flung the whole word to the back. Why?

Because < compares bytes, not letters

Elixir strings are UTF-8 encoded binaries (a string really is a sequence of bytes — the same boxed-binary representation from the earlier rungs). The < operator does a structural comparison: it walks the two byte sequences and compares numeric values; the first differing byte decides. It is fast and predictable — exactly why it powers ordered sets and Enum.sort/1 — and completely blind to what the bytes mean.

ASCII letters are one byte each (0–127): a = 97, o = 111. But á is not ASCII — in UTF-8 it's two bytes, 0xC3 0xA1 = 195, 161. So the first byte of "álien" is 195, versus 111 for "office". 195 > 111 → the entire string sorts later. Compare them byte-for-byte:

Structural vs semantic, restated

Structural comparisonSemantic comparison
"How is this laid out as bytes?" — what <, >, Enum.sort/1 do. Cheap, total, the basis for collections & algorithms. Blind to meaning. "What do these mean, and how should they be ordered for this purpose?" For text that's collation order, where á files next to a, before o.

The same split shows up beyond strings. You can structurally compare a Time with a Date — they're just terms in the type lattice Ch.4.1 — but it's semantically meaningless, which is why those modules provide their own compare/2 returning :lt/:eq/:gt and refuse nonsense comparisons.

The practical takeaway For user-facing text — names, titles, anything alphabetical — don't rely on raw </> or plain Enum.sort/1; accented and non-Latin characters will scatter to the wrong places. Use a collation-aware approach (the Unicode Collation Algorithm), via a sort key that normalises/transliterates accents, or a dedicated Unicode collation dependency, so "álien" lands beside "alien" the way a human alphabetising a list would expect.
Why this is the right place to end The behavior only makes sense because you now hold the lower model: a string is a binary, a binary is bytes, and < compares the representation. The surface anomaly dissolves into an implementation fact you can see. That's the whole point of the descent — the model isn't trivia, it's what turns "weird bug" into "obviously, of course."
