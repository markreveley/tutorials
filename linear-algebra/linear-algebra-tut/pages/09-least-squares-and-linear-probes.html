Module 2 · Subspaces and fitting

Least squares: fitting a direction to labels

A linear probe is a direction chosen so that projections match a label as closely as possible in the squared-error sense. The normal equations say the mismatch is orthogonal to the data.

The problem

You have an activation matrix X of shape (n, d) and a label y of length n, one number per token. You want a direction w and an offset b such that X w + b is as close to y as possible. Least squares defines “close” as the sum of squared differences, Σᵢ (xᵢ · w + b − yᵢ)², and asks for the w and b that minimise it. The solution is a linear probe; its projection x · w + b is the probe’s score for a token. For a binary label the score is a number that should be near 1 on positives and near 0 on negatives.

The geometry: residual orthogonal to the data

Centre X and y so that the offset disappears, and look at the columns of X as d vectors in ℝⁿ, one per dimension, each listing that dimension’s value across the n tokens. The prediction X w is a linear combination of those columns, so it lies in their span, a subspace of ℝⁿ. The closest point in a subspace to y is the orthogonal projection of y onto it, by the same picture as lesson 03 with the roles swapped: the subspace is the thing projected onto, y is the thing projected. The residual y − X w is therefore orthogonal to every column of X, which written out is the normal equations.

minimise ‖X w − y‖²
Xᵀ (y − X w) = 0, i.e. XᵀX w = Xᵀ y (normal equations)
with ridge λ: (XᵀX + λI) w = Xᵀ y

XᵀX is the (d, d) matrix of dot products between columns, the uncentred cousin of the covariance matrix of lesson 10, and Xᵀy lists the dot product of each column with the label. In code, numpy.linalg.lstsq solves the problem directly and, when there are more columns than rows so that many w fit exactly, returns the one with the smallest norm. [numpy: lstsq] The exercise asks you to centre, call lstsq, and recover the offset as ȳ − x̄ · w.

On the fixture

With 768 dimensions and only 211 training tokens, there are more unknowns than equations. Any label can be fitted exactly, so the training fit is meaningless; what matters is the held-out part. The lab splits the 301 tokens 70/30 with a fixed seed, fits on the training part, and reports both.

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py probe --label is_digit
label is_digit: 45 ones of 301; train 211 tokens, held out 90; ridge 0.0
least-squares direction w: shape (768,), |w| = 0.0374, intercept b = 0.034
train: correlation(pred, y) = 1.000; mean squared error 0.0000
held out: correlation(pred, y) = 0.986; mean squared error 0.0045
normal equations check on train: max |Xc.T @ residual| = 6.43e-13 (residual is orthogonal to every column)
cos(w, mean-difference direction) = 0.734; held-out correlation using the mean-difference direction instead: 0.935

The training correlation is exactly 1.000 with zero error: interpolation, as predicted. The held-out correlation is 0.986, which is the number that means something, and the mean squared error of 0.0045 says the held-out scores are within about 0.07 of the 0/1 label on average. The normal-equations check is the geometry made numerical: the residual’s dot product with every centred column is zero to twelve decimal places. The final line compares the least-squares direction with the mean-difference direction of lesson 03: their cosine is 0.734, so they are related but different, and on held-out tokens the probe does better (0.986 against 0.935). Least squares uses the covariance between dimensions to cancel what the classes share; the mean difference does not.

Ridge: a penalty on the norm

When w is not unique, or when the data have directions of tiny variance that lstsq will exploit, add λ‖w‖² to the objective. The normal equations gain λ on the diagonal, the solution becomes unique for any λ > 0, and the direction shrinks toward zero as λ grows. The practice command below uses λ = 100 on a harder label. Choosing λ is a model-selection question whose only honest answer is held-out performance; the statistics course covers the logic.

In interpretability work

Linear probes are the standard first test of “is X linearly represented at this layer”, and ARENA’s probing section trains them on a model playing a game. [ARENA] The two numbers to demand from any probe are the held-out score and the baseline for that score (lesson 14). The training score of an overparameterised probe is 1 for any label, including random ones.

Pitfall

Treating the probe direction w as “the feature direction the model uses”. Least squares finds the direction that best predicts the label under squared error on this sample; the model may compute with a different direction that correlates with it, and lesson 08 showed that removing w does not necessarily erase the label.

Check your understanding

The probe fits the 211 training tokens with correlation exactly 1.000. If you shuffled the digit labels among the training tokens and refitted, what training correlation would you get, and what held-out correlation would you expect?

Reveal the reasoning

Training correlation 1.000 again: with 768 unknowns and 211 equations, lstsq fits any labels exactly, including shuffled ones. Held-out correlation near zero, because there is no relation between the shuffled labels and the activations to carry over. The pair (1.000 train, ≈0 held out) is the signature of an interpolating fit of noise, and it is why lesson 14 reports the permutation baseline for the held-out score and ignores the training score.

Practice

Fit a ridge probe for “first character is upper case” with λ = 100. Compare train and held-out correlations with the digit probe.

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py probe --label capitalised --ridge 100
What to look for

The training correlation will be below 1 now (ridge stops exact interpolation) and the held-out correlation well below the digit probe’s 0.986, somewhere around 0.68 in the transcript. Capitalisation after block 6 is less linearly readable on 211 tokens than digit-ness, or needs more data; the probe cannot say which. The mean squared error line tells you how far scores sit from the labels on average.
