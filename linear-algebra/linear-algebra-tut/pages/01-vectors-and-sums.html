Module 0 · Vectors

Vectors, addition, and scaling

Add two activations coordinate by coordinate, scale one by a number, and you have everything a residual stream does between layers.

Two dimensions first

Take u = (3, 1) and v = (1, 2). Their sum is (3 + 1, 1 + 2) = (4, 3): add matching coordinates. Scaling u by 2 gives (6, 2): multiply every coordinate. Subtracting is adding the negative: u − v = (2, −1). Draw u and v as arrows from the origin and the sum is the far corner of the parallelogram they make; scaling stretches an arrow along its own line; a negative scale flips it. Nothing else is allowed. There is no multiplication of two vectors into a vector, and there is no division.

u + v = (u₁ + v₁, …, u_d + v_d)
c·u = (c·u₁, …, c·u_d)
2u − 3v is a linear combination of u and v

A linear combination is a sum of scaled vectors. Every vector you will meet in this course that is not raw data is a linear combination of something: a direction is a combination of basis vectors, a projection is a scalar times a direction, a residual-stream activation is a combination of layer outputs. The two operations, addition and scaling, are the whole of what “linear” means, and lesson 04 defines a linear map as a function that respects exactly them.

The residual stream is a sum

In a transformer each block reads the current activation, computes something, and adds its result back. After block 6, the activation for a token is the token embedding plus the position embedding plus the output of each of the seven attention layers and seven MLP layers before it. The authors of the transformer-circuits framework put it directly: “The residual stream is simply the sum of the output of all the previous layers and the original embedding.” [Elhage et al. 2021] That sentence is why vector addition is the first lesson. The 768 numbers in the fixture are a sum of fifteen other 768-vectors, and much of interpretability consists of asking which of the summands is responsible for which part of the total.

The fixture holds only the total, so the lab demonstrates addition with what it has: two tokens’ activations, their sum, a scaled copy, their difference, and the difference between one token and the mean of all tokens. Each is a legitimate vector in the same 768-dimensional space, and the last one, the centred activation, is the object PCA will work on in lesson 11.

Check the first coordinate by hand: −2.182 + 5.984 = 3.802, printed as 3.803 because the stored values carry more digits than shown. That is the entire operation, repeated 768 times. The last line is a reminder that the fixture is stored in float16 and loaded as float32: addition of these particular values happened to be exact, but in general floating-point sums are not, and when a check prints 10⁻¹³ rather than 0 that is rounding, not a counterexample.

Difference vectors are directions

The difference u − v points from v to u. If u is a digit token and v is not, the difference is a first, crude guess at a “digit direction”: it is the vector you would add to v to arrive at u. Averaging many such differences gives the mean-difference direction used in lesson 03, which is simply the mean activation of the digit tokens minus the mean activation of the others, itself a linear combination of all 301 activations with coefficients +1/45 and −1/256.

Subtracting the mean from every token is the same idea applied to the whole batch. Coordinates that are large for every token, such as index 447 from lesson 00, are large in the mean, and subtracting the mean removes what every token shares. What remains is where tokens differ, and only differences can track a label. This is why the covariance and PCA lessons begin by centring.

Shapes

Addition needs equal shapes: (768,) + (768,) is allowed, (768,) + (64,) is a type error. Numpy additionally lets you add a (768,) vector to every row of a (302, 768) matrix, which is how X − X.mean(axis=0) centres a whole batch in one line; the rule is called broadcasting, and the shape of the result is (302, 768). When a broadcast surprises you, write the shapes out before trusting the numbers.

In interpretability work

Steering a model by adding a scaled vector to its residual stream is vector addition and scaling and nothing else: x′ = x + c·d. The interesting questions are which d, which c, and what changes downstream. The arithmetic is this lesson.

Pitfall

Reading “the model adds a feature” as if features were ingredients that keep their identity in the sum. Once two vectors are added you cannot recover either from the total alone. Separating them again is the subject of lessons 03, 08 and 11, and it needs extra information: a direction, a label, or a variance criterion.

Check your understanding

The mean activation of the 301 tokens after position 0 has first coordinate 0.317 and token 12’s is −2.182. If you add the mean-centred activation of token 12 to the mean-centred activation of token 40, and then add the mean back, what do you get, and why does it matter?

Reveal the reasoning

You get (u − m) + (v − m) + m = u + v − m, which is not u + v. Centring is linear but not idempotent under addition: summing centred vectors double-subtracts the mean. In practice this means you must decide once whether you are working with raw or centred activations and keep every vector in the same convention; mixing the two produces a vector shifted by a multiple of the mean, and the mean is large in exactly the coordinates that dominate norms.

What to look for

The two “harbour” activations are not identical; context after block 6 has changed them, so the difference is not zero. But its coordinates are smaller than those of the difference between a number and a noun. Lesson 02 turns that impression into a number with the cosine similarity.
