Module 4 · Calculus and rigor

Is this correlation real?

A direction’s projection correlates with a label. Before believing it, see what shuffled labels and random directions produce on tokens the direction never saw.

Correlation is a cosine

The correlation between two sequences of n numbers a and b is the cosine similarity of their centred versions: subtract each mean, then divide the dot product by the two norms. It lies between −1 and 1, is unchanged by scaling or shifting either sequence, and is zero when the centred sequences are orthogonal. Everything you learned about cosines in lesson 02 applies: two sequences that are both dominated by a shared component correlate highly for that reason alone, and a correlation of 0.4 means nothing until you know what unrelated sequences of this kind produce.

corr(a, b) = (a − ā) · (b − b̄) / (‖a − ā‖ ‖b − b̄‖)

In this course a is the projection of each token onto a direction and b is a label. The lesson-09 probe reached correlation 0.986 with the digit label on held-out tokens. Three questions decide whether that is a finding.

Three questions, three baselines

Was the direction fitted on these tokens? If so, the correlation is a training score and lesson 09 showed it is 1.000 for any label whatsoever. Only held-out tokens count. What correlation would shuffled labels produce? Permuting the label among the held-out tokens destroys any relation while preserving the label’s proportions and the projection’s distribution; the spread of correlations over many shuffles is the permutation null, and an observed value well outside it is unlikely to be chance. What would a random direction produce? Projecting the held-out tokens onto directions drawn at random tells you how much correlation with this label the geometry of the activations hands out for free, which is a different question from the permutation one and, on this fixture, has a very different answer.

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py correlation --label is_digit
label is_digit; direction fitted on 211 tokens; correlation train 1.000, held out 0.986
permutation baseline on the held-out tokens (200 shuffles of the label): mean 0.002, 97.5th percentile 0.219, max 0.364
shuffles with |r| >= observed held-out |r|: 0 of 200
random-direction baseline (held out, 200 directions): mean 0.008, max |r| 0.620
the same projection correlated with other labels on held-out tokens: after_period 0.000, starts_with_space -0.022, capitalised -0.067

The permutation null for 90 held-out tokens with 13 or so digits among them has mean 0.002 and a 97.5th percentile of 0.219; its largest value in 200 shuffles was 0.364. The observed 0.986 is outside anything the shuffles produced, so the relation between this direction’s projection and the label is not an accident of these 90 tokens. The random-direction baseline tells a different story: of 200 random directions, the best reached a held-out correlation of 0.620 with the digit label. Digit tokens form a tight cluster (lesson 02 found cosines above 0.7 among them), and any direction with a chance component along the cluster’s offset from the rest correlates with the label somewhat. So “a direction correlates 0.6 with is_digit” would not be a finding on this fixture, and “0.986” is a finding mainly because it is far above 0.620. Which baseline you need depends on the claim: shuffled labels test “is there a relation at all”, random directions test “is this particular direction better than nothing”.

The last line asks whether the direction is specifically about digits or about something correlated with digits in this passage. Its projection has correlation 0.000 with after_period, −0.022 with starts_with_space and −0.067 with capitalised on the held-out tokens. That rules out three confounds; it does not rule out the ones you did not list, and in a passage where every number followed the word “counted”, a direction reading “follows counted” would pass every test here.

What this is and is not

This lesson is the geometric minimum: a held-out split, a permutation null, a random-direction null, and a look at alternative labels. It does not give you confidence intervals on the correlation, a correction for having tried several directions or labels before reporting one, or a sample-size calculation for how many held-out tokens you need to tell 0.6 from 0.98. Those are statistics, and the statistics course has a lesson on correlation, permutation tests and multiple-comparison control for screening many candidate directions; take this lesson as the question and that one as the method. The rigor framing of the roadmap applies here exactly: write down the held-out correlation that would count as success, and the baseline that would also pass if the claim were empty, before you look.

In interpretability work

“Feature 1234 fires on digits” is a correlation claim between a projection and a label. The questions are the same: which tokens were held out, what do shuffled labels give, what does a random direction give on this data, and what else does the projection correlate with. A claim that cannot report the first and either of the next two has not been tested.

Pitfall

Using the permutation null alone when the label marks a tight cluster. It will say almost any direction is “significant”, because almost any direction separates a cluster a little, and 0.3 is far outside the shuffle distribution. The random-direction baseline is the one that calibrates “how good is this direction”.

Check your understanding

The permutation 97.5th percentile is 0.219 and the random-direction maximum is 0.620. A candidate direction scores 0.45 on held-out tokens. Is it a digit direction?

Reveal the reasoning

It passes the permutation test, so its projection is related to the label beyond chance. It fails the random-direction comparison: random directions reach 0.62 on this fixture, so 0.45 is worse than what geometry gives away for free, and the direction has no demonstrated advantage over noise as a digit detector. The honest report is “related to digit tokens, but no better than a random direction on this layer and sample”. If the direction came from an external hypothesis, that is still informative; if it was selected from many candidates, it is not even that.

Practice

Run the baselines for the after-period label with 100 shuffles. Compare the held-out correlation with the two nulls.

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py correlation --label after_period --perms 100
What to look for

The held-out correlation is lower than for digits (lesson 09 found about 0.73 to 0.78 depending on ridge) and the random-direction maximum is lower too, because sentence-initial tokens do not cluster as tightly as numbers. Judge the direction by the gap between its score and the random-direction maximum, not by the permutation percentile alone.
