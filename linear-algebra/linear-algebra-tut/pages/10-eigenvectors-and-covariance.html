Module 3 · Spectra

Eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and the covariance matrix

A symmetric matrix has a set of orthonormal directions it merely stretches. The covariance matrix of an activation batch is symmetric, and its eigenvectors are the directions along which the tokens spread most.

Directions a map only stretches

Apply a square matrix A to many vectors and most of them change direction. An eigenvector v is one that does not: A v = λ v, the output is the input scaled by the eigenvalue λ. The 3Blue1Brown chapter on eigenvectors shows a shear leaving one line fixed; that line is the eigenvector direction. For a general matrix eigenvalues can be complex and eigenvectors need not be orthogonal, and this course avoids that case entirely, because the matrices whose spectra interpretability cares about are symmetric, A = Aᵀ, and for those everything is clean: the eigenvalues are real, and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, a full basis of the space in which A is diagonal.

A v = λ v (eigenvector v, eigenvalue λ)
for symmetric A: A = V Λ Vᵀ, V orthonormal, Λ diagonal
x · (A x) = Σᵢ λᵢ (x · vᵢ)² for a unit vector x

The last line is the reading that matters. Write x in the eigenbasis as coordinates cᵢ = x · vᵢ; then the quadratic form x · A x is Σ λᵢ cᵢ². A symmetric matrix is a weighted sum of squared projections, with the eigenvalues as weights and the eigenvectors as the directions projected onto. The largest eigenvalue is the largest value of x · A x over unit vectors, attained at its eigenvector. Everything in lessons 11 and 12 is an instance of this sentence.

The covariance matrix

For an activation matrix X of shape (n, d), subtract the mean row to get X_c and form C = X_cᵀ X_c / (n − 1), a (d, d) matrix. Entry (i, j) is the covariance between coordinates i and j across tokens; the diagonal holds the variances. C is symmetric because covariance is. And for a unit direction u, u · C u is the variance of the projection coefficients X_c u, which you can verify by expanding: u · X_cᵀ X_c u / (n − 1) = ‖X_c u‖² / (n − 1). So the eigen-reading applies: the top eigenvector of C is the direction along which the tokens have the largest variance, and its eigenvalue is that variance. The lab computes C from the 301 non-outlier tokens and checks each claim.

variance along the top eigenvector 289.4; along neuron 447 55.7; along neuron 0 5.71

The top eigenvalue, 289.4, is the variance of the tokens along the top eigenvector, and the last line confirms it directly by projecting and taking the variance. Along neuron 447, the large coordinate from lesson 00, the variance is only 55.7, and along neuron 0 it is 5.71. The direction of greatest spread is not a neuron. The sum of all eigenvalues equals the sum of the 768 per-coordinate variances, 3,822.7, because the trace of a matrix is basis-independent; that total is what “variance explained” in lesson 11 is a fraction of. At least 468 eigenvalues are zero because 301 centred rows span at most 300 dimensions: the covariance of a small sample is always low-rank, and the zeros are a statement about the sample size, not the model.

The penultimate line previews lesson 12: the singular values of X_c, squared and divided by n − 1, are the eigenvalues of C. The SVD of the data matrix and the eigendecomposition of its covariance are the same computation, and in practice one uses the SVD because it avoids forming the (768, 768) product and squaring the rounding error.

Computing it

numpy.linalg.eigh is the routine for symmetric matrices. It returns eigenvalues in ascending order, so the largest is last, and eigenvectors as columns of the second output. [numpy: eigh] It reads only one triangle of the input and never checks symmetry, which means that handing it a non-symmetric matrix silently decomposes a different matrix; the general routine eig exists for the non-symmetric case and returns complex numbers when it must. For covariance matrices and for the products AᵀA that lesson 12 uses, eigh is the right call.

In interpretability work

Any time a (d, d) symmetric matrix appears, its eigenvectors are a basis in which it is a list of weights, and the top few eigenvectors are where its action is concentrated. Covariance, Gram matrices of feature directions, and the symmetric part of a bilinear form such as W_QK are the common cases.

Pitfall

Reading the top eigenvector of the covariance as “the most important direction for the model”. It is the direction of largest variance in this batch of tokens, which depends on the text, the tokens included, and whether position 0 was excluded. Lesson 11 shows the first component flipping meaning entirely when one row is added back.

Check your understanding

The covariance eigenvalues sum to 3,822.7 and the top one is 289.4. Someone reports “the first component explains 7.6% of the variance”. Someone else, using all 302 tokens including position 0, reports 88.9%. Both are correct computations. What does the disagreement tell you?

Reveal the reasoning

That the total variance and its top direction are properties of the sample, and that one token with norm 3,047 contributes a squared deviation from the mean of order 3,000² ≈ 9 million against about 300 × 3,800 ≈ 1.1 million from all the other tokens together. With token 0 included the top eigenvector points from the mean toward token 0 and explains almost everything; without it the spectrum is spread across many directions. Neither number is wrong; neither is a fact about GPT-2 in general. Report which tokens went in.
