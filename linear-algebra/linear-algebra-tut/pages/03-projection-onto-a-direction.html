Projection onto a direction: the central move

Projecting every activation onto one candidate direction turns a (302, 768) matrix into 302 numbers. Most of interpretability is deciding whether those numbers mean something.

What a projection is

Take a vector v and a nonzero direction d. Write d̂ = d / ‖d‖ for the unit vector along d. The projection of v onto the line through d has a coefficient and a vector:

coefficient c = v · d̂ = v · d / ‖d‖ (a scalar; signed length along d)
projection p = c · d̂ (a vector on the line through d)
residual r = v − p (a vector, with r · d = 0)

In two dimensions, v = (3, 4) onto d = (1, 0): d̂ = (1, 0), c = 3, p = (3, 0), r = (0, 4). Onto d = (1, 1): d̂ = (1, 1)/√2, c = 7/√2 ≈ 4.95, p = (3.5, 3.5), r = (−0.5, 0.5). Check that r · d = −0.5 + 0.5 = 0 in the second case: the residual is always perpendicular to the direction, because the projection took exactly the component along d and nothing else. Check also that ‖p‖² + ‖r‖² = 24.5 + 0.5 = 25 = ‖v‖²: Pythagoras, since p and r are perpendicular. These two identities are the tests in labs/test_linalg.py and the checks the lab prints.

The coefficient does not depend on the length of d, because d̂ does not: projecting onto (10, 10) gives the same c, p and r as projecting onto (1, 1). The dot product v · d, by contrast, scales with ‖d‖. When a paper reports “the projection of activations onto the feature direction”, it matters whether they normalised d; if they did not, the numbers are v · d and are only comparable between tokens, not between directions.

A scalar per token

Apply the coefficient formula to every row of the activation matrix at once: X d̂ has shape (302, 768) times (768,) giving (302,), one number per token. This is the central move of the course and of a great deal of interpretability practice: a candidate direction, a batch of activations, a scalar per token, and then the question of whether the scalars line up with something you can name.

The simplest candidate direction for a binary label is the mean-difference direction from lesson 01: the mean activation of tokens with the label minus the mean activation of tokens without it. The lab builds it for the digit label and projects every token (position 0 excluded).

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py project --label is_digit

Read it top to bottom. The direction has norm 35.5, so the gap between the class means along d̂ is 35.5 by construction: the mean coefficient of digit tokens is 25.68 and of the others −9.82, and 25.68 − (−9.82) = 35.5. That is not evidence of anything; it is what the mean difference does. The evidence, such as it is, lies in the ordering: the six highest coefficients are all number tokens, the lowest are all nouns, and token 12 sits at 19.71, on the digit side. The last two lines are the Pythagoras and orthogonality checks on real data: 19.71² + 86.29² = 7,833.8 = 88.51², and r · d is −2·10⁻¹³, zero up to rounding.

Notice the sizes. The coefficient along d is 19.71 while the residual has length 86.29. The digit direction accounts for a small part of token 12’s activation; most of the vector is elsewhere. “This token projects strongly onto the digit direction” is a statement relative to other tokens, not a statement that the token is mostly digit-ness.

What this does not establish

The projection was computed on the same tokens used to build the direction. A mean-difference direction will always separate the means of the two groups it was built from; the question of whether it separates new digit tokens from new non-digit tokens is lesson 09 and lesson 14. And the label was chosen by you. The projection can confirm or refute “d tracks is_digit”; it cannot tell you that d tracks nothing else. The lab’s correlation command, later, projects the same direction against the other labels for that reason.

In interpretability work

Almost every “feature” you will read about, from a probe direction to a sparse-autoencoder decoder column, is used by projecting activations onto it and reading the scalars. When you see a plot of “feature activation” over tokens, you are looking at c = x · d̂ (or x · d) per token, possibly thresholded. Ask how d was chosen, whether it was normalised, and which tokens were held out.

Pitfall

Comparing projection coefficients across two directions with different norms without normalising. x · d doubles if d doubles. The coefficient x · d̂ does not.

Check your understanding

The mean coefficient of digit tokens along d̂ is 25.68 and of non-digit tokens −9.82; the gap equals ‖d‖ = 35.5. Would a random label with 45 ones produce a gap of zero?

Reveal the reasoning

No. Build the mean-difference direction for any label and the gap between the class means along it equals ‖d‖ by construction, which is positive unless the two means coincide exactly. With 45 random tokens against 256 in 768 dimensions the sample means differ by chance, so ‖d‖ is positive and the gap is positive. The gap is therefore not a measure of whether the label is represented. What distinguishes a real label is that the direction built on some tokens separates other tokens, and that the gap is large relative to the spread within each class. Lesson 14 computes both.

Practice

Build the mean-difference direction for “token follows a full stop” and project. Look at which tokens score highest and at token 100 (' 12', a digit, not after a period).

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py project --label after_period --token 100
What to look for

The highest coefficients should be sentence-initial words, and the ordering is less clean than for digits: after_period has only 23 positive tokens, so the direction is estimated from fewer examples. Token 100 should be on the negative side. Compare its coefficient with the gap ‖d‖ printed on the first line to judge how far from the boundary it sits.
