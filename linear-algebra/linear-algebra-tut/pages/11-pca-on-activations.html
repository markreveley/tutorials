PCA on an activation matrix

Principal component analysis finds the orthonormal directions of greatest variance and reports how much each explains. On the fixture it finds position; what it cannot find is anything you did not put in the batch.

What the components are

PCA takes a centred data matrix X_c of shape (n, d) and returns orthonormal directions v₁, v₂, … such that v₁ is the unit direction of largest variance, v₂ the unit direction of largest variance among those orthogonal to v₁, and so on. By lesson 10 these are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix in decreasing eigenvalue order, and in practice they are the right singular vectors of X_c (lesson 12). The score of a token on component i is its projection coefficient (x − x̄) · vᵢ, so PCA is lesson 03 applied with directions that the data chose. Variance explained by component i is its eigenvalue λᵢ divided by Σ λⱼ, the fraction of the total variance lying along it; the cumulative sum tells you how much of the data’s spread k components capture.

v₁ = argmax over unit u of Var(X_c u); v₂ = the same with u ⟂ v₁; …
explained(i) = λᵢ / Σⱼ λⱼ
scores = X_c Vₖᵀ, shape (n, k); rank-k reconstruction = scores Vₖ + x̄

How to read it

The scree. The variance-explained numbers 0.076, 0.057, 0.055, 0.042, … fall slowly. There is no elbow: no small number of components after which the rest are negligible. Ten components give 38%, fifty give 73%, a hundred give 88%. A scree plot of these numbers would be a gentle slope, and the honest summary is that the variation among these 301 tokens is spread across many directions. Had the first component explained 60% and the second 3%, the matrix would be nearly rank one in its variation, and one direction would describe the batch. It does not.

The first component. Its largest neuron weight is −0.809 at index 87, so it is mostly but not only one coordinate. The tokens at its two ends are telling: the low end is the last sentence of the passage (tide, cargo, crossing, calm), the high end comes from the first two paragraphs (households, metres). Its score correlates with token position at −0.923. In this batch the direction of greatest variance is, to a first approximation, how far into the text the token is. That is a fact about this fixture that you could not have known before looking, and the right response is to ask whether it survives a different passage, not to announce that GPT-2 has a position direction.

The other labels. Component 1 has correlation only 0.200 with the digit label; component 2 has 0.528. The digit signal that a probe reads with held-out correlation 0.986 is not concentrated in the top component and is only partly in the second. Variance and label-relevance are different criteria, and PCA optimises the first. Lesson 15 makes the comparison exact: the probe direction w has cosine 0.055 with component 1.

What PCA does not tell you

It does not tell you what the components mean. A component is a direction of spread, labelled afterwards by looking at which tokens score high and low, and the labelling is your inference, not the algorithm’s output. It does not find directions relevant to a label you have in mind unless that label happens to drive variance. It is not invariant to the choice of tokens: run pca --keep-first and the first component explains 88.9% of the variance and points at token 0, because that single row’s squared distance from the mean dwarfs everything else (lesson 10’s check question). It is not invariant to scaling of coordinates: if you standardise each of the 768 coordinates to unit variance you get different components, and neither choice is canonical. And a component explaining 7.6% of variance is not “unimportant”: the digit direction explains far less and predicts its label almost perfectly.

In interpretability work

PCA is a first look at a batch of activations and a baseline for any claimed direction: if your feature direction is just the top principal component, you have found the dominant variation in your prompt set, which may be position, sentence length, or a formatting artefact. Report the variance explained, the tokens that went in, and whether outliers were excluded.

Pitfall

Running PCA on uncentred activations. Without subtracting the mean, the first “component” is the direction of the mean vector itself, which in this layer is large (lesson 02), and the variance-explained numbers describe the mean rather than the spread.

Check your understanding

Component 1 correlates with position at −0.923. Would you report “GPT-2 layer 6 encodes position along its top principal component”?

Reveal the reasoning

Not from this. The batch is one passage of 301 tokens, and anything that varies smoothly through a passage, such as topic, sentence structure or the running context, is confounded with position. The prediction test is the way to find out: before looking, state that the same component direction (not a refitted one) will correlate with position on a different passage, choose the correlation you would call a success, and run it. If it does, the claim is about the direction; if the correlation only reappears after refitting PCA on the new passage, it is about the method.

Practice

Include token 0 and rerun with five components. Read the variance explained and the two ends of component 1.

python3 labs/linalg_lab.py pca --keep-first --k 5
What to look for

The first component explains 88.9% of the variance and its score is extreme for one token, position 0, with everything else bunched near the other end. One outlier row has become the whole first component. This is the practical reason the other commands exclude position 0, and the general reason to compute norms before PCA.
