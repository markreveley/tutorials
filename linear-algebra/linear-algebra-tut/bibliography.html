Annotated bibliography

Primary papers, institutional references, and official method documentation, with the verified scope of each source.

These sources were opened and checked during authoring on 2026-10-20. The notes identify the scope verified; they do not claim that an entire source was independently reproduced. The local labs validate the supplied implementations on the real fixture.

Elhage et al. 2021

Elhage, Nanda, Olsson et al. (2021), A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits

Verified quotes: "The residual stream is simply the sum of the output of all the previous layers and the original embedding"; "the residual stream doesn't have a 'privileged basis'; we could rotate it by rotating all the matrices interacting with it, without changing model behavior"; W_O W_V and W_Q^T W_K "can always be thought of as individual, low-rank matrices"; "The OV and QK matrices are extremely low-rank. They are 50,000 × 50,000 matrices, but only rank d_head (64 or 128)"; the SVD of W_OV: right singular vectors describe the subspace "read in", left singular vectors the subspace written to.

Elhage et al. 2022

Elhage, Hume, Olsson, Schiefer et al. (2022), Toy Models of Superposition

Verified quotes: toy models "investigate how and when models represent more features than they have dimensions. We call this phenomenon superposition"; "Superposition simply means that there are more features than dimensions"; "Although it's only possible to have n orthogonal vectors in an n-dimensional space, it's possible to have exp(n) many 'almost orthogonal' (< ε cosine similarity) vectors in high-dimensional spaces", citing the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma. The course quotes the definitions and the geometric fact; it makes no claim about which features GPT-2 holds in superposition.

3Blue1Brown

Grant Sanderson, Essence of linear algebra (video series, 16 chapters), 3blue1brown.com

Verified on 2026-10-20 by opening each lesson page: chapter numbers and titles 1 Vectors, what even are they?; 2 Linear combinations, span, and basis vectors; 3 Linear transformations and matrices; 4 Matrix multiplication as composition; 5 Three-dimensional linear transformations; 6 The determinant; 7 Inverse matrices, column space, and null space; 8 Nonsquare matrices as transformations between dimensions; 9 Dot products and duality; 10 Cross products; 11 Cross products in the light of linear transformations; 12 Cramer's rule, explained geometrically; 13 Change of basis; 14 Eigenvectors and eigenvalues; 15 A quick trick for computing eigenvalues; 16 Abstract vector spaces. The series has no chapter on SVD, PCA, least squares, or gradients.

numpy: svd

NumPy reference: numpy.linalg.svd

Verified: returns U, S, Vh with singular values "sorted in descending order"; with full_matrices=False the shapes are (M, K) and (K, N) with K = min(M, N) and a == (u * s) @ vh.

numpy: lstsq

NumPy reference: numpy.linalg.lstsq

Verified: computes x that minimises the Euclidean 2-norm of b − a x; "If there are multiple minimizing solutions, the one with the smallest 2-norm is returned"; residuals is an empty array when rank(a) < N or M <= N.

numpy: eigh

NumPy reference: numpy.linalg.eigh

Verified: for a real symmetric (or Hermitian) matrix, returns eigenvalues "in ascending order" and eigenvectors as columns; only one triangle of the input is read, so it does not check symmetry.

numpy: einsum

NumPy reference: numpy.einsum

Verified: "Evaluates the Einstein summation convention on the operands"; np.einsum('ij,jk', a, b) is matrix multiplication, np.einsum('i,i', a, b) is the inner product, np.einsum('ii', a) is the trace.
