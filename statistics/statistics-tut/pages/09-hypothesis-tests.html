Module 2 · Compare systems

Tests, p-values, and practical decisions

Learn what a test actually asks, then separate statistical evidence from the size of change you care about.

A test needs a specified null

A null hypothesis is a statistical claim you assess against observations. For the paired binary comparison, a useful null says the two types of discordance are equally likely. Conditional on a task being discordant, B wins with probability one half. Independent discordant pairs then give a binomial count.

In our example, B wins 36 of 60 discordant pairs. A two-sided exact McNemar test computes how unusual an imbalance at least this large would be under the symmetric null. It can be calculated as twice the fair-binomial probability of 24 or fewer wins, capped at one. This is the binomial test specialized to a fair null. [SciPy: binomial]

Read the direction of the probability

A p-value is a probability of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one, under the specified null and assumptions. A value of 0.155 does not mean there is a 15.5% chance the versions are equal or an 84.5% chance B is better. It is a statement about possible data under a model, not a posterior probability of that model.

The ASA’s guidance explicitly distinguishes p-values from hypothesis probabilities and effect importance. [ASA] A small p-value can motivate scrutiny of the null model, but it does not identify which assumption failed. A grader change, selection difference, or confounded run order can also produce a difference.

The two decision errors

A Type I error rejects a true null. A test’s alpha, often chosen as 0.05, is a planned upper error rate under its assumptions, not a universal definition of scientific truth. A Type II error fails to reject when a specified alternative is true. Power is one minus that second error probability.

Power depends on the particular alternative. A design can reliably detect a twenty-point change while usually missing a two-point change. A failure to reject zero can therefore reflect limited information, rather than equality. Say “inconclusive for this effect” when that is what the data support.

Compare with practical margins

Suppose switching costs make gains below three points uninteresting. An interval from +0.2 to +1.0 points may provide strong evidence of a positive effect while remaining too small to motivate the switch. Conversely, an interval from −2 to +12 leaves a worthwhile gain plausible but unresolved.

For noninferiority, define an acceptable loss margin m before observing data. With B−A, you seek evidence that the difference is above −m, using a predeclared one-sided analysis. For equivalence, you seek evidence the difference lies within both margins. A common two-one-sided-test procedure at alpha 0.05 checks containment of a 90% two-sided interval in those bounds under the matching model. Merely including zero in an ordinary 95% interval proves neither claim. [Equivalence tests]

In eval work

Put three things in the decision discussion: observed effect, uncertainty range, and the practical threshold. If they point in different directions, state the unresolved tradeoff rather than hiding it behind a significance label.

Pitfall

Changing a two-sided test to one-sided after seeing the direction of the result changes the error properties. Set the direction and decision rule in advance.

Check your understanding

A candidate has an interval of −4 to +4 points. Your equivalence margin is ±1 point. Can you declare equivalence because zero is inside?

Reveal the reasoning

No. The interval allows changes much larger than the equivalence margins. You need an analysis capable of ruling out practically important differences in both directions, using a predeclared equivalence procedure.

Practice

Write a decision sentence for each interval: [−2,+12], [+0.2,+1.0], and [+4,+8] percentage points. Assume a three-point gain is needed to justify switching and all comparisons are otherwise valid.

What to look for

The first leaves both harm and worthwhile benefit plausible. The second supports a small positive change that falls short of the required gain. The third supports a gain above the stated threshold under its interval assumptions. Cost, critical regressions, and validity still belong in the decision.
