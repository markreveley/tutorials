Module 4 · Measure the measuring instrument

When a model says “80% confident”

Distinguish predicting a label from predicting a useful probability of that label.

Does the number match its frequency?

A probability forecast is calibrated on a population if events assigned a given probability occur at about that frequency. Among many comparable cases assigned 0.8 success probability, roughly 80% should succeed. This is a property across cases under stated conditions, not a guarantee about an individual case. [Calibration]

A model’s confident tone is not a probability forecast. If you ask it for a numerical confidence, define the event and collect the forecast before showing the outcome. A confidence about “the answer seems plausible” is not necessarily a probability of passing your rubric.

Build a reliability diagram

Group forecasts into prespecified probability bins. For each bin, compare the average forecast with the observed fraction of successes. Display the count, too: a bin with three outcomes cannot support the same precision as one with three thousand.

This constructed example suggests overconfidence in both bins: forecasts exceed observed success. It does not establish what happens in unsampled bins or populations. Bin boundaries, sparse bins, and averaging can hide local patterns. A summary calibration error that changes when you change bins is not a complete assessment.

Score probabilities directly

Brier score = average of (forecast probability − binary outcome)²

For each case, square the forecast’s distance from zero or one, then average; lower is better. With eight successes and two failures, a constant forecast of 0.9 gives (8 × 0.1² + 2 × 0.9²)/10 = 0.17. A constant 0.8 forecast gives 0.16 for those same outcomes.

Log loss instead scores −[y log(p) + (1−y) log(1−p)]. Correct confident predictions get small losses; confidently wrong predictions get large losses. A probability of zero for an event that occurs gives infinite loss mathematically. If software clips probabilities for numerical reasons, document the clipping convention.

These are proper scoring rules: under the scoring setup, the expected score is optimized by an honest probability forecast. A proper score captures more than calibration; discrimination and inherent outcome uncertainty also contribute. A lower Brier score therefore need not mean calibration alone improved. [Calibration]

Calibration and useful decisions

A constant forecast equal to the population success rate can be calibrated while failing to distinguish easy from hard cases. To route uncertain work to humans, you need both useful discrimination and reliable probabilities in the region where the decision threshold lies.

Evaluate the actual abstention policy: coverage is the fraction handled automatically, and selective error is the error fraction among those handled. A system can make its accepted subset look better by handling almost nothing. Show both quantities, along with performance on critical slices.

If you fit a calibration transformation, fit it on development data and assess it on independent held-out data. Selecting the transformation and reporting performance on the same observations can be optimistic. Population shift can also change calibration after deployment.

In eval work

Keep forecast probabilities with task IDs and outcomes. For a model comparison, analyze paired loss differences and preserve the same grouping structure you used for success-rate comparisons.

Pitfall

Equating verbal certainty, classification accuracy, calibration, and discrimination. They measure different aspects of behavior and can move independently.

Check your understanding

A forecaster always says 70%, and exactly 70% of cases succeed. Is it useful for deciding which cases need review?

Reveal the reasoning

It may be calibrated in aggregate, but it cannot rank cases because it gives every case the same forecast. Review routing needs information that separates higher- and lower-risk cases, assessed with coverage and selective error on appropriate data.

Practice

For a task that fails, calculate Brier loss for forecasts 0.6, 0.8, and 0.99. Then repeat for a task that succeeds. Explain what confidently wrong forecasts cost.

What to look for

On failure the losses are 0.36, 0.64, and 0.9801. On success they are 0.16, 0.04, and 0.0001. A proper scoring rule rewards useful confidence while penalizing unsupported certainty across repeated cases.
