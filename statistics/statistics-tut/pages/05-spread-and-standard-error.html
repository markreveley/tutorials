Module 1 · Understand uncertainty

Spread of outcomes versus uncertainty of an average

Standard deviation and standard error answer different questions. Learn to recognize both in an eval report.

Measure the scatter

Take four hypothetical task scores: 0, 0, 1, 1. Their mean is 0.5. Deviations from the mean are −0.5, −0.5, +0.5, +0.5. Squaring makes them all 0.25, so the squared deviations sum to 1.

Sample variance s² = 1 / (4 − 1) = 1/3
Sample standard deviation s = √(1/3) ≈ 0.577

For independent observations from a common distribution with finite variance, dividing by n−1 corrects the usual sample variance for having estimated the center from those same observations. The square root returns to the original score scale. Standard deviation describes how individual scores vary around their mean.

Now repeat the whole measurement

Imagine independently sampling four new tasks and calculating another mean. Repeat that entire process many times. The means themselves have a distribution. Its standard deviation is the standard error of the mean. You usually have one dataset, so you estimate that standard error.

Estimated SE(mean) = s / √n

Take the observed standard deviation and divide by the square root of the independent sample count. The formula requires the relevant independence and common-population assumptions. NIST’s mean interval uses this quantity together with a suitable critical value. [NIST: means]

For the four scores, estimated SE is about 0.289. This is a tiny sample: mechanically calculating an SE does not establish that a normal interval will have good coverage. The next lesson develops that distinction.

The binary shortcut

A Bernoulli outcome has population variance p(1−p). At p = 0.7, that is 0.21, with standard deviation about 0.458. The mean of 100 independent trials has theoretical standard error √(0.21/100) ≈ 0.0458, or 4.58 percentage points. With 400 trials it is about 2.29 points.

When p is unknown, substituting the observed pass fraction gives the familiar plug-in formula √(p̂(1−p̂)/n). The hat means “estimated from data.” It differs slightly from using the sample standard deviation with its n−1 denominator. Both become close for large n; neither fixes dependence or extreme small-sample behavior.

What the square root costs

To halve this standard error while holding the outcome distribution and independence assumptions fixed, you need four times as many observations. Ten times the sample size reduces it by about √10, not by ten. This is why a small effect can be expensive to distinguish reliably.

Do not confuse adding distinct tasks with rerunning the same tasks. New tasks can reduce uncertainty from task selection. More attempts estimate each observed task’s behavior more precisely. Both can help, but they act on different parts of the variation; lesson 11 separates them.

In eval work

If a chart labels error bars “±1 SD,” ask whether it means variation across task scores, repeated run means, or bootstrap estimates. These describe different distributions. Label the object whose spread is being displayed.

Pitfall

Duplicating every observation four times can trick a naive formula into halving its reported SE. No new evidence was collected. The apparent precision comes from a false independence assumption.

Check your understanding

A report says task-score SD is 0.4 across 100 independent tasks. Does that imply uncertainty of 40 percentage points around the mean?

Reveal the reasoning

No. The estimated SE of the mean is 0.4/√100 = 0.04, or four percentage points, under the independence model. Individual outcomes remain much more variable. An interval adds a method and coverage assumptions.

Practice

Predict the ratio of the two theoretical SEs. Run both experiments and compare the empirical SD of estimates with the theoretical SE.

python3 labs/stats_lab.py sample --trials 100 --seed 7
python3 labs/stats_lab.py sample --trials 400 --seed 7
What to look for

The theoretical SE halves. The empirical SD is close but not identical because the simulation itself uses finitely many experiments. Increasing experiments improves the simulation estimate; increasing trials improves each simulated eval.
