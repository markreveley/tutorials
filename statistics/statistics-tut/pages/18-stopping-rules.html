Module 5 · Make defensible decisions

Peeking and stopping rules

Checking progress is ordinary engineering; making a statistical decision whenever a favorable threshold appears is a different experiment.

A valid test at one time is not valid at every time

You plan to evaluate 1,000 tasks, but you check the p-value after every twenty and stop as soon as it falls below 0.05. Under a null effect, estimates wander. Some paths cross a threshold early and later return. Selecting the first favorable crossing creates more opportunities to reject than the fixed-endpoint test accounted for.

The observations are not independent across looks: the first forty include the first twenty. You cannot repair the problem by pretending each dashboard refresh is a fresh independent study. The stopping rule is part of the procedure whose error rate must be assessed.

See the mechanism without eval complications

The lab generates iid normal observations with true mean zero and known standard deviation one. At each planned look it computes z = sample mean × √n. Under the null, the fixed-time statistic is standard normal, so a two-sided threshold near ±1.96 has a 5% rejection rate.

It compares two rules: reject only at n = 1,000, or reject if any of the fifty looks crosses the threshold. The second rule has a higher false-positive frequency despite no true effect. The simulation uses normal data so the demonstration is about stopping, rather than a questionable approximation for small binary samples.

Three defensible paths

  • Fixed endpoint: collect the predeclared sample and make the confirmatory decision there.
  • Planned interim analyses: allocate the error budget across specified looks with a valid design.
  • Sequential inference: use methods designed for permitted ongoing monitoring, such as an appropriate confidence sequence.

A confidence sequence aims for simultaneous coverage over time, rather than coverage at one chosen time. It has its own assumptions and usually pays for that broader guarantee with wider intervals. Howard and colleagues develop time-uniform constructions; this course introduces their purpose rather than implementing a generic sequential test. [Confidence sequences]

Stopping for a broken run is still allowed

You can inspect logs, watch spend, detect a broken grader, or halt a damaging rollout. Statistical discipline does not require continuing an invalid or unacceptable operation. Preserve the reason for stopping and avoid interpreting the truncated data with an unchanged fixed-sample claim.

Stopping for futility can also be planned: perhaps no plausible remaining outcome would justify the cost. That is a design question. Choosing an unplanned stopping rule after seeing a promising effect and describing it as an ordinary fixed-size test is the failure to avoid.

Does Bayesian updating make this disappear?

Under a correctly specified Bayesian model and a suitable ignorable stopping process, updating can be coherent under optional stopping. That does not automatically grant a threshold-based launch policy a chosen frequentist error rate. Selective reporting, prior tuning, dependency errors, and a changed population remain problems. Specify what guarantee you want before choosing the method.

In eval work

Write both the run-stopping rule and the decision rule in the protocol. “Stop at 1,000 eligible tasks unless infrastructure integrity fails; assess the primary contrast once” is reviewable.

Pitfall

Repeatedly adding another batch only when the result narrowly misses significance. The final sample size then encodes what previous batches showed.

Check your understanding

Can you check halfway through an eval whether outputs are empty without invalidating everything?

Reveal the reasoning

Yes. Inspecting integrity and taking a documented operational action is different from claiming a fixed-sample significance guarantee after outcome-driven stopping. Record any change, repair the experiment if needed, and interpret the resulting data under the actual procedure.

Practice

Predict which rejection rule fires more often even though the simulator’s mean is zero. Run it, then explain why fifty correlated looks still create a problem.

python3 labs/stats_lab.py peek --experiments 2000 --seed 7
What to look for

The any-look rule rejects more frequently. Correlation between looks does not make them one preselected endpoint. This command demonstrates an invalid rule; it does not provide a sequentially valid analysis for your own eval.
