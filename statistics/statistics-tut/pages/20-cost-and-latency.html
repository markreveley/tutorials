Module 5 · Make defensible decisions

Cost, latency, and tradeoffs

A practical eval decision usually has several objectives, each requiring a clear denominator and observation policy.

What resource does a success consume?

Success rate, total spend, and elapsed time describe different aspects of a system. A useful cost measure is total spend divided by total delivered successes. If 100 tasks consume 50 hypothetical cost units and 70 succeed, cost per success is 50/70 ≈ 0.714 units.

The numerator includes failed attempts, retries, and verification within the declared system boundary. Averaging cost only on successful tasks omits resources spent on failures. If a version achieves zero successes, cost per success is undefined rather than a useful finite performance estimate.

A ratio is not an average of ratios

Imagine two batches: ten cost units buy ten successes, and ninety cost units buy thirty successes. Total cost per success is 100/40 = 2.5. Averaging the two batch ratios gives (1+3)/2 = 2. Equal batch weighting and pooled success weighting answer different questions.

For uncertainty in total-cost/total-success, resample the independent units and recompute both totals inside each draw. Do not attach an interval for mean cost to the ratio and ignore uncertainty in successes. Sparse successes can make ratio estimates unstable.

Latency needs its own definitions

Specify when the clock starts and stops: scheduler enqueue to final artifact, first request to response, or only model computation. Include or separately report time spent waiting, retrying, validating, and cleaning up. A faster model call can coexist with a slower end-to-end workflow.

Report central tendency and tail behavior with counts. For 100 measurements, a p99 is determined by roughly the top one or two values under common conventions. It cannot establish a stable tail distribution. A bootstrap of extreme sample quantiles can be fragile, especially when slow cases are censored or removed.

For paired tasks, analyze task-level cost and latency differences when appropriate, keeping related groups together. A candidate’s slow tasks may be exactly the baseline’s slow tasks; separate unpaired summaries discard that structure. Time-related shared load may create additional dependence beyond task identity.

Look for a frontier, then state preferences

VersionObserved successMean cost/taskComment
A75%1.0Baseline
B80%1.2Higher success and cost
C74%1.3Worse than A on both observed objectives

This hypothetical table has a tradeoff between A and B. On the observed point estimates, C is dominated by A. A Pareto frontier contains options for which no alternative is at least as good on every objective and better on at least one. Uncertainty can change which dominance claims are justified.

There is no universal exchange rate between a percentage point of success and a unit of cost. If you build a utility score, document its weights and test sensitivity to them. Keep critical guardrails outside a compensatory average when the product requirement demands that.

In eval work

Compare systems at an explicitly matched resource budget, or show the success–cost curve across budgets. A candidate with five retries is a different procedure from a baseline with one attempt.

Pitfall

Optimizing successful-only latency and then celebrating a version that times out on its hardest tasks. A conditional timing metric needs a completion-rate companion.

Check your understanding

B’s successful tasks are cheaper on average, but B fails much more often. Is B cheaper to deliver a successful result?

Reveal the reasoning

You cannot tell from successful-task cost alone. Sum all relevant spend and divide by delivered successes under a common workload and retry policy. Report uncertainty and the number of successes; the ratio may worsen even as successful-only cost falls.

Practice

Construct two five-task result tables in which the successful-only cost favors B but total cost per success favors A. Include failures in the ledger.

What to look for

One example: A spends one unit on each task and succeeds on four, giving 5/4 = 1.25. B spends 0.5 on its one success and one unit on each of four failures, giving 4.5 per success. B’s successful-only cost is lower while delivered success is more expensive.
