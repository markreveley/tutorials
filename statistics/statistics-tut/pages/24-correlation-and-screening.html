Module 6 · Screen many candidates

Correlation, permutation, and thousands of candidates

Measure how two columns move together, test it by shuffling, and count how many candidates would pass by chance alone.

Two numbers for one question

A new kind of eval question appears when you look inside a model. You have a candidate unit: a neuron, a direction in activation space, or a feature from a dictionary. You compute its activation on many inputs. You also have a label for each input, such as whether it mentions a date, or a predicted activation written down from an explanation. The question is whether the activation column and the label column move together, and whether that co-movement is more than noise.

The Pearson correlation answers the first part on a linear scale. For n paired observations (x₁,y₁)…(xₙ,yₙ) with means x̄ and ȳ:

r = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ) / √[Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)²]

Each term in the numerator is a product of two deviations: positive when both values sit on the same side of their means, negative when they sit on opposite sides. The denominator scales the sum so the result has no units and lies between −1 and +1. Multiplying every activation by ten, or adding a constant, leaves r unchanged. Zero means no linear co-variation, which is weaker than independence: a U-shaped relation can have r near zero. [SciPy: Pearson]

The Spearman correlation replaces each column by its ranks (1 for the smallest value, n for the largest, ties sharing the average of the ranks they occupy) and then computes Pearson’s r on the ranks. It measures any monotone relation and ignores how far apart the values are, so one extreme activation cannot dominate it. When there are no ties, a shortcut gives the same number from the rank differences dᵢ = rank(xᵢ) − rank(yᵢ): ρ = 1 − 6Σdᵢ² / [n(n²−1)]. [SciPy: Spearman]

The ranks do not care that the last label is 20 rather than 8. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions. Report which one you used and why.

Shuffle the labels: the permutation test

Is r = 0.778 more than eight random pairs would produce? One null hypothesis says the labels carry no information about the activations: any way of attaching the eight labels to the eight activations was equally likely. Under that null you can generate the whole null distribution yourself. Keep the activations in place, reorder the labels, recompute r, and repeat.

With n = 8 there are 8! = 40,320 orderings, few enough to enumerate every one. The two-sided permutation p-value is the fraction of orderings whose |r| is at least the observed 0.778. The lab command below enumerates them: for Pearson the fraction is 0.0030; for Spearman 0.0046. The exact meaning is modest and precise: if the labels were exchangeable, about three orderings in a thousand would show a linear co-variation this strong. It does not say the probability that the association is real, and it says nothing about how large the association is. [SciPy: permutation test]

For larger n, enumeration is impossible and the lab draws random orderings instead. With K random relabelings and c of them at least as extreme, it reports p = (c + 1)/(K + 1), counting the observed ordering as one draw; this keeps the smallest reportable p at 1/(K + 1) rather than zero. The test needs no normality assumption, which is why it suits activations, which are usually skewed with a long upper tail. For comparison, the normal-theory p-value for the same eight pairs is 0.0231, nearly eight times larger, because the formula does not know that the single large label is one point rather than a normal tail.

The permutation test does assume that the observations are exchangeable under the null. Tokens from the same document, or inputs that share a template, are not. If the dependence matters, permute at the level of the independent unit, as lesson 11 did for repositories: shuffle labels between documents, not between tokens within a document.

Thousands of candidates

A model has thousands of neurons, and a screen tests every one against the same label. Suppose 3,000 candidates and none of them has any real association. At a threshold of p < 0.05, each one has a 5% chance of passing by chance, so the expected count of false positives is 3,000 × 0.05 = 150. The lab simulation with seed seven produces 151. Any report that announces “we found 150 label-sensitive neurons” from such a screen has found exactly what noise promises.

Two corrections take different goals. Bonferroni tests each candidate at α/m, here 0.05/3,000 ≈ 0.0000167, and bounds the probability of even one false rejection across the family. [NIST: Bonferroni] Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate: the expected fraction of rejections that are false. Sort the m p-values, find the largest i for which p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ (i/m)·q, and reject the i smallest. The procedure was introduced by Benjamini and Hochberg in 1995 and is valid for independent tests or positively dependent ones. [Benjamini–Hochberg 1995] [SciPy: FDR]

The largest i that passes is 5, so Benjamini–Hochberg rejects the five smallest p-values at q = 0.05. Bonferroni at 0.05/8 = 0.00625 rejects only the first two. Note the step-up logic: a p-value can be rejected even if it fails its own threshold, as long as a larger one further down the list passes.

In the all-null simulation both rules reject nothing. Now plant thirty candidates with a real association strong enough to matter (--real 30 --effect 1). In that run, 178 candidates pass the raw threshold, of which 148 are noise; Bonferroni keeps 25 of the thirty real ones and no noise; Benjamini–Hochberg keeps 29 and no noise. The generator knows which were planted, so the lab can print the realized false-discovery count. A real screen cannot, which is why the guarantee is an expectation over screens, not a certificate for one. [Multiple testing]

Neurons in one layer are not independent tests: they co-activate on the same inputs. Positive dependence is covered by the Benjamini–Hochberg guarantee; arbitrary dependence is not, and the Benjamini–Yekutieli variant or a permutation of the whole screen are the usual responses. Match the dependence structure before trusting a count.

In eval and interpretability work

State the family before screening: how many candidates, which label, which statistic, which rule. “3,000 neurons, Spearman against the date label, Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.05, labels permuted within documents” is a reviewable procedure. A list of the 40 highest correlations is a search result.

Pitfall

Reading a tiny permutation p-value as a large effect, or as the probability the association is real. With 50,000 tokens, r = 0.04 can have p below 0.0001 and still explain almost nothing about what the unit does.

Check your understanding

A colleague screens 2,000 features against a label, keeps the 95 with p < 0.05, and calls them the label’s feature set. Before looking at any of them, what do you expect that list to contain?

Reveal the reasoning

Under the null alone, 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 features would pass. Ninety-five is what pure noise delivers, so the list is uninformative as it stands. Apply a family-level rule, then confirm survivors on data not used for the screen. If Benjamini–Hochberg rejects nothing, the honest report is that the screen found no feature distinguishable from noise at that sample size.

What to look for

Your pencil values should match 0.7776 and 0.9048. The all-null screen passes about 150 of 3,000 at p < 0.05 and both corrections reject nothing. With planted signal, Benjamini–Hochberg keeps more real candidates than Bonferroni, because it bounds a fraction rather than any single error. Seeds other than seven change the exact counts; the expectation does not.
